The Error-Correction Moat

Satya Nadella says companies pay for AI twice. He is right about the risk, and only half right about the fix. This guide explains what an organization actually needs to protect, and how to build an "epistemic harness" that helps it learn faster than its competitors.

AuthorKarl
Co-AuthorShubham Mehrish
First publishedJuly 14, 2026
RevisedNovember 10, 2026
Written forLeaders and non-technical teams

The argument in five points

  1. Nadella has found a real problem. To get value from an AI model, a company has to show it how the company works, and that know-how can leak to the model provider.
  2. But a stockpile of data is not the moat. Logs of past prompts and corrections are a record of yesterday's answers. The advantage that lasts is how quickly an organization can notice a mistake, understand it and fix it.
  3. Knowledge grows in two loops at two speeds. Inside the company, fixes happen in days. Inside the model provider, new models arrive every few months. The harness is the layer where the two loops meet.
  4. Every workaround is a hypothesis. Each fix should come with a written reason and a test that could prove it wrong. When a better model makes the fix unnecessary, the fix is removed.
  5. People remain essential. Their job is to choose which problems matter, define what "good" looks like, and catch the subtle errors that pass every automated check.

Words used in this guide

ModelThe AI system rented from a provider (such as OpenAI, Anthropic, Google or an open-source project). It is general-purpose and knows nothing specific about your company.
AgentA model set up to carry out tasks, such as reading files, using tools and taking actions, rather than only answering questions.
HarnessEverything your organization wraps around the model so that it does useful, safe work: instructions, tools, permissions, memory and tests.
SkillWritten instructions that teach agents how your organization does a specific job. It captures your know-how, not a model weakness.
Workaround (shim)A fix added to the harness because the model can't yet do something reliably on its own.
EvalA test that checks whether the AI's work is good enough, the way an exam checks a student.
Held-out testA test case kept aside on purpose and never used to build a fix, so that it gives an honest verdict on the fix.
Part 1 · The starting point

1. The worry Nadella named

On July 12, 2026, Microsoft's CEO Satya Nadella published an essay on X called "The Reverse Information Paradox." It reached millions of readers within days, and it named a worry that many executives already had.

The name refers to a 1962 idea from the Nobel economist Kenneth Arrow. Arrow noticed that it is hard to sell information: to prove it is valuable, you have to reveal it, and once it is revealed the buyer has it for free. In that version of the problem, the seller is at risk.

Nadella argues that AI reverses who carries the risk:

"…the buyer risks giving away knowledge, just in order to use what they bought."

Satya Nadella, "The Reverse Information Paradox," July 12, 2026

His point is that a company pays for AI twice. It pays once in money. It pays again in know-how, because a model only becomes useful once you show it your processes, your judgment calls and your corrections. He calls this trail "intelligence exhaust," and he says it leaks gradually, "trace by trace, correction by correction, eval by eval." If the learning flows only toward the provider, he warns, the value ends up with whoever owns the AI infrastructure rather than with the people who created the knowledge.

His fix is a firm "trust boundary" around the company's own data and learning, organized as five priorities:

01
Control
Own your memory, tests, feedback and decisions, including your own definition of a "good" result.
02
Capability
Tune and train models on your own workflows so that learning builds up inside the company.
03
Choice
Don't depend on a single AI provider; be able to switch.
04
Cost
Send each task to the most efficient model that can do it well.
05
Compound
Build a learning loop that keeps growing in value and keeps that value inside the firm.

Commentators were quick to point out that the essay comes from the head of a company that sells exactly this kind of cloud infrastructure, so readers should keep that context in mind. The problem it describes is real regardless.

Part 1 · What holds up

2. What Nadella gets right: corrections are the valuable part

Nadella's most important observation is that the valuable thing is not the questions employees type. It is the corrections: the moments when an experienced person looks at the AI's answer and says "no, that's wrong here, and here's why."

That matches one of the best-tested ideas about how knowledge grows. The philosopher Karl Popper argued that we never prove ideas right. We make guesses, look for their mistakes and keep what survives. In those terms:

The model's answer is a guess. A person's correction is the criticism that shows where the guess fails. Corrections carry local, hard-won judgment that no general-purpose model has.

The economist Friedrich Hayek made a related point: much of the knowledge that matters is "knowledge of the particular circumstances of time and place," held by people on the ground and impossible to gather in one central place. Nadella doesn't cite Hayek, but his worry fits that idea: a company's edge lives in its local details.

How the value escapesProving the information is worth paying for means revealing it.Making the model useful means feeding it your context and corrections.

Part 1 · What needs correcting

3. Where the fix goes wrong: three mistakes

Nadella's diagnosis is strong. The cure he proposes, which amounts to walling off the exhaust and letting it pile up, rests on three assumptions that don't hold.

Mistake 1: Treating knowledge as something you fill a bucket with

Popper had a name for the idea that minds, or organizations, become wise by passively collecting observations: the "bucket theory of the mind." He set it against a "searchlight" theory, in which knowledge comes from actively asking questions, proposing explanations and testing them.

"Intelligence exhaust" is bucket thinking. It assumes that if enough prompts, logs and corrections are poured into a secure container, an advantage will form on its own. The physicist David Deutsch, who extends Popper's ideas, adds that raw data contains no explanations. A log of past corrections records which problems were solved yesterday. It doesn't contain the understanding needed for the problems that arrive tomorrow.

A company that locks down its logs while treating its people as prompt-typists will soon have nothing worth locking down.

Mistake 2: Assuming knowledge is zero-sum

The paradox assumes that if a provider learns something from your corrections, you have lost it. Knowledge doesn't work that way. When your engineer corrects a model, the engineer still knows what they knew, and still has the ability to spot the next mistake. A provider can capture a record of where the searchlight pointed yesterday. It can't capture the searchlight.

That doesn't mean nothing can leak. The table below separates what can be copied from what can't:

Can be copiedWorth guarding closelyCan't be copied
Documents, logs, individual examples and past answers. Your definition of "good" (your private tests), your current context, and the failures you've learned from. These steer every future fix. Your people's ability to choose the right problems, notice subtle errors and explain why something failed.

Mistake 3: Assuming that using a model means training it

"Exhaust leaks" sounds automatic, but it isn't. Sending a request to an AI provider doesn't by itself mean the provider trains on it; that depends on the contract and the settings. Even when training is allowed, a lot has to happen before your corrections improve someone's model: rights have to be cleared, data selected and checked, and a training run actually performed. The useful question is not "will it leak?" but "which gate decides what leaves, and who controls it?" Section 7 covers that gate.

Part 2 · The better model

4. How knowledge actually grows: guess, test, keep what survives

Popper summed up the growth of knowledge as a four-step cycle. It works for a scientist, a company, or a company and its AI agents together:

P1
Problem
Something goes wrong, the market shifts, or leaders set a new operational or strategic goal.
→
TT
Guess
An agent or a person proposes a fix, a plan or a new way of working.
→
EE
Test against reality
Tests, reviewers and real-world results: what customers and the market did, what worked and what didn't.
→
P2
New problem
What survives goes live and exposes the next, better problem.

↺ and the cycle starts again

Two lessons follow for organizations.

Problems are not only failures. A problem can be a mistake to fix, but it can just as well be a new operational goal, a strategic bet, a competitor's move or a shift in what customers want. Most of the valuable problems are chosen, not stumbled on.

Testing is not only internal. Automated tests and reviewers are the fast, cheap form of criticism. The final judge is reality: whether customers adopt it, whether the numbers move, whether the operation runs better. Signals from the market, both what worked and what didn't, are criticism too, and the harness should capture them.

It doesn't matter who proposes the guess. Popper held that the source of an idea is irrelevant; only the testing counts. An AI agent can suggest a fix just as well as an employee can, within the brief it has been given. Setting that brief, choosing which problems are worth solving and judging the results remain the scarce human contribution (more on why in section 12).

The test is the step that matters most. The advantage goes to the organization that turns this cycle fastest and most honestly, not to the one that has stored the most data.

A running example

An insurance company uses an AI agent to draft claim decisions. An experienced adjuster notices that the agent keeps misreading the flood clause in older policies, whose wording changed a few years ago. That's the problem.

A team member proposes a small add-on that checks the policy's issue date and pulls the right version of the clause. That's the guess.

Before it goes live, it is tested against past claims, including awkward ones that weren't used to build it. That's the attempt to break it. It passes, goes live, and the next hurricane season brings new edge cases. That's the new problem.

We'll come back to this example throughout the guide.

Part 2 · The two loops

5. Two loops, two speeds, and the harness between them

The same cycle runs in two places at very different speeds.

  • The enterprise loop turns in days. Your people and agents find problems, try fixes and keep what works, without changing the AI model itself.
  • The model loop turns in months. AI providers find gaps that show up across many customers, train new models, test them, and release them.

The two loops meet at the harness. Many of the fixes your organization makes are workarounds for things today's models can't do on their own. When a later model learns to do that thing cleanly, the workaround is no longer needed and should be removed.

The example, continued

Eight months later the provider releases a new model that reads older policy wording correctly without help. The team runs the claims workflow with and without the flood-clause add-on. Results are just as good without it, so the add-on is removed. The adjusters' time goes to the next problem: hurricane claims that involve several overlapping policies.

Keep the two loops separate in your thinking:

  • A task that looks successful is not automatically a good example to train a model on.
  • Rights, privacy, careful selection, checking and an actual training run all have to happen before your work improves anyone's model.
  • Test cases a model has already trained on no longer give an honest verdict. Some tests must stay protected and never go into training.
Part 3 · The harness

6. What the epistemic harness is, in plain terms

The harness is your organization's machinery for correcting errors. Its job is to make every mistake cheap to spot, explain, contain and undo.

It is not just a set of clever prompts or an "agent platform." Those are parts of it. A useful comparison is an aviation safety system: a checklist that says what to do, controls that physically limit what can be done, a flight recorder that captures what happened, and incident reviews that turn each failure into a better checklist. Aviation didn't become safe by collecting more flight data. It became safe because it made learning from every error routine.

Hypothesis registryKeeps a card for every skill and workaround, and the rest of the harness acts on those cards (see Rule 1 below). Nothing runs without one.Do we know why each piece of our AI setup exists, and does the system enforce it?
Authority layerLimits what agents can touch, keeps workflows separate, and makes actions reversible and logged.If an agent does something wrong, how bad can it get and how fast can we undo it?

Part 3 · Rules of the harness

7. Four rules that make the harness work

Rule 1: Every fix is a hypothesis, with a card

Deutsch offers a test for a good explanation: you shouldn't be able to change its details without breaking it. His example is an ancient myth about the seasons. It "explains" winter, but you could swap any detail for another and the story would still work, which means it explains nothing.

AI workarounds often fail the same test. A prompt tweak that "just works," for reasons nobody can state, can be changed at random and no one can tell what will happen. A pile of such tweaks becomes a system nobody dares to change. The remedy is a short card for each fix:

Hypothesis card · Flood-clause add-on · v2Live
Problem
The agent misreads the flood clause in policies issued before the wording change.
Why it should work
It checks the issue date first and uses the matching clause. The date check is the essential part: without it, the add-on fails.
What would prove it wrong
Older-policy claims still come out wrong with the add-on switched on, or come out right with it switched off.
Retirement test
At each new model release, run 200 set-aside claims with and without the add-on. Remove it if results, cost and speed are as good without it.
Covers for · Review by
Current model's weakness with old policy wording · re-check in six months
May touch
Policy documents (read only). No payments.
Owner · Proposed by
Claims operations lead · drafted by an AI agent, reviewed by a senior adjuster

If nobody can fill in the "why it should work" line, nobody understands the fix yet. That is the first useful finding.

Skills, workarounds and safety controls all get cards, but they are not treated the same. All three are guesses that could be wrong, so all three are registered and tested. What differs is what makes each one obsolete:

SkillYour organization's own know-how: how you handle a claim, what your pricing rules are, how your team writes a contract.A change in your business, your market or your rules. A better model doesn't make it obsolete, because the model can't know your way of working unless you tell it.Against real outcomes, and at its review date.
Workaround (shim)A fix for something today's model can't do reliably on its own, like the flood-clause add-on.A better model. It is expected to be removed eventually.A retirement test at every new model release.
Safety controlLimits on what agents may touch, keeping workflows separate, the ability to undo.Nothing about the model. These stay even when models improve.Regular drills to check that the controls actually work.

In practice the line can blur. A single add-on often mixes your know-how with a fix for a model weakness. When that happens, split it into two cards, so the workaround part can be removed later without losing the know-how.

The card is not paperwork; the harness runs on it. Each field is read by another part of the system:

So the registry is the harness's control panel: it decides what may run, with what permissions, how it is tested, and when it is reconsidered.

What needs a card, and what doesn't. Cards for literally everything would be bureaucracy, and paperwork that nobody reads is worse than none. The rule of thumb is that something needs a card when it is reused and could matter:

Needs a cardDoesn't need one
Anything agents load and act on repeatedly: skills, workarounds, standing instructions, tool connections.

Anything that can touch money, customers, records or other systems, or that can't easily be undone.

Every test that is used to judge other fixes.

Every safety control.		 One-off questions and drafts a person reviews before using.

Experiments in a sandbox that can't affect real work. They get a card only if they are promoted into everyday use.

Reference documents. These carry a source, an owner and a review date instead.

Match the size of the card to the stakes. A short card with four lines (problem, why it should work, what would prove it wrong, owner) is enough for most things. The full card, with permissions, undo steps and a retirement test, is for work that can cause real harm or that covers for a model gap. Agents can draft cards and people approve them, so the cost stays low. And the card system follows its own rule: if cards are being filled in after the fact and never read when something changes, cut them back to the fields that are actually used.

Rule 2: Tests should try to break things, and the tests need checking too

A test encodes a belief about what "good" looks like, and that belief can be wrong. When people (or agents) aim at a score instead of the real goal, the score stops meaning anything; economists call this Goodhart's law. A test suite that only ever confirms success is not doing its job. Good testing includes awkward cases designed to cause failure, cases the fix was never built for, and people who regularly check whether the tests' verdicts match reality.

That also gives a fair way to tell real knowledge from a one-off fix: a fix counts as knowledge only if it also works on cases it wasn't built for. A fix that only solves the case that prompted it is a one-off, and its card should say so, with an early review date.

Note that this is a different question from the skill-or-workaround question in Rule 1. That one asks what a fix makes up for: a gap in the model, or your own know-how. This one asks how far the fix reaches. Either kind can turn out to be a one-off, and either kind can turn out to be real knowledge.

Rule 3: Keep the evidence, especially the failures

Record what each fix led to in practice: what worked, what didn't, and how customers and the market responded. Failures deserve special care, because a failure tells you exactly where an idea broke, while a success only tells you it hasn't broken yet. Store every failed fix together with the evidence that killed it, and have people check that log before proposing something new. Keeping only the examples that passed a test sounds sensible, but it quietly bakes in whatever blind spots the test has.

Rule 4: Control means mistakes are cheap to undo

Popper suggested that the question "who should rule?" matters less than "how can we remove a bad ruler without disaster?" For AI the equivalent question is: how quickly can a bad skill, agent or decision be spotted, contained and reversed?

  • Give each agent only the permissions its current task needs.
  • Sort actions into reversible and irreversible. Irreversible ones need a person's approval.
  • Practice undoing things, the way you'd run a fire drill, rather than assuming it will work.
  • Keep these safety controls even when models improve. A better model doesn't make a mistake any easier to undo.

This also answers a common fear: being responsible doesn't mean a person has to review every action. Human attention goes to irreversible actions and new fixes. Small, reversible, well-logged actions can run on their own.

Part 3 · Why the work never ends

8. The frontier keeps moving, and the harness moves with it

Better models don't make the harness unnecessary. They move it. In areas the model has mastered, the harness gets thinner because workarounds are removed. At the frontier it gets richer, because newly possible tasks need new scaffolding.

Deutsch's phrase fits: problems are inevitable, and problems are soluble. The harness is never finished, and that's expected. Each solved problem exposes a newer, more valuable one.

Part 4 · Strategy

9. The four pillars of a lasting advantage

The first edition of this essay proposed four pillars of defensibility. Here they are revised to match the harness. Each pillar says what to build and why it is hard for a competitor or a provider to copy.

Pillar 1
Living context, memory and skills
What it is
An organized, current picture of how your organization works, plus written skills that tell agents how to do specific jobs. Every entry has a source and an expiry date, so outdated knowledge gets checked or dropped.
Why it's hard to copy
A rented model starts knowing nothing about you. Accurate, current context supplied at the moment of work is what makes it useful, and it only stays accurate if your people keep it so.
Pillar 2
Your own tests: owning the definition of "good"
What it is
Private tests built from your real work, including set-aside cases and deliberately awkward ones, that every fix and every new model must pass.
Why it's hard to copy
If your tests live in a vendor's dashboard, you'll drift toward the vendor's idea of success. Your own tests let you switch models safely, catch regressions and aim at your business goals. This is the part of the "exhaust" most worth guarding.
Pillar 3
Boundaries you control
What it is
Clear decisions about where models run, what agents may touch and what may leave the organization. That includes the authority layer, the training gate, and the freedom to use more than one provider. Running open models on your own machines is one option here, suited to the most sensitive work, but it isn't the only one.
Why it's hard to copy
It turns "will our knowledge leak?" from a worry into a decision your organization makes, records and can reverse. Contracts and settings matter as much as hardware.
Pillar 4
People who explain, and a culture of criticism
What it is
People whose job is to choose problems, define good outcomes, write sharp tests and challenge the AI's output, working in a culture where reporting an error is rewarded rather than punished.
Why it's hard to copy
Knowledge is created by people explaining and criticizing, not distilled from logs. If people are punished for mistakes, they hide them, and the error-correcting loop stops. A culture that welcomes bold guesses and honest criticism is the one advantage nobody can rent.

Part 4 · Is it working?

10. How to tell whether it's working

Measure how quickly and cheaply the organization corrects errors, not how busy the agents are.

Track thisIn plain termsIt warns you about
Time to detectHow long a serious mistake goes unnoticed.Errors that slip past every check.
Time to correctHow long from spotting a problem to a tested fix going live.A slow enterprise loop.
Repeat rateHow often a fixed problem comes back.One-off fixes mistaken for real ones.
Cost per accepted resultTotal AI and review cost, divided by the results people actually accept.A system that costs more than the errors it catches.
Workarounds retiredHow many workarounds are removed at each new model release without problems.A harness that only ever grows.
Test disagreementHow often people overrule a test's verdict.Tests that have drifted from reality.

Don't count as success: number of agents, tasks run, tokens used or skills built. A growing pile of skills is a cost unless old workarounds are being retired at a similar pace. These measures are themselves hypotheses about what matters, so revisit them whenever a new model arrives.

How the loop breaks

Stale context
Memory that was once true now misleads. Check: give entries an expiry date and re-verify them.
Gamed tests
The team optimizes the score, not the outcome. Check: rotate tough cases and audit the tests.
Untouchable workarounds
Fixes nobody dares remove. Check: run retirement tests on a schedule.
Overbuilt machinery
The harness costs more than the errors it catches. Check: watch cost per accepted result.
Part 4 · Getting started

11. Where to start

Build what makes mistakes cheap before you build what makes agents more capable. Each step is useful on its own and prepares for the next.

  1. Put limits and recording in place first. Restrict what agents can touch and log what they do, so any later mistake is visible and reversible.
  2. Write a card for every existing skill and workaround. The ones nobody can explain are your first finding.
  3. Start an evidence log as soon as there are cards to test: what each fix led to, and every failure with what proved it wrong.
  4. Build your own tests. Start with past failures and permission checks, then add set-aside cases, then tough rotating cases and human review of the tests themselves.
  5. Formalize how problems are chosen and ranked once failures arrive faster than people can sort them by hand.
  6. Run your first retirement check at the next model release.
  7. Build the training gate last, and only if you plan to share data for training or train your own models. Until then it stays closed, and that's a perfectly valid choice.

Pilot on one workflow where mistakes matter but can be undone. Expand only after the repeat rate on that workflow starts to fall.

Questions to ask your team on Monday

Which three problems would most change our results if our AI handled them well, and who chose them?
What does a "good" result look like for each, written down before anyone builds a fix?
Do our tests live with us or with a vendor? When did a test last catch a real problem?
If an agent made a costly mistake today, how long until we noticed, and could we undo it?
Which of our AI workarounds could we explain to a newcomer? Which could we safely remove?
What do our provider contracts actually say about training on our data, and who checked?
Part 4 · Testing this argument

12. How this argument could be wrong

In keeping with Popper, this essay is a set of guesses, not a forecast. These are the observations that would show it is mistaken:

Our claimWhat would prove it wrong
Cards make changes saferTeams with cards see problems recur as often as teams without them.
The advantage comes from correction speedMistakes are caught faster, but accepted results don't improve. The real bottleneck is elsewhere, probably in choosing problems.
The harness thins as models improveAlmost no workarounds can be retired across two model releases.
Working on set-aside cases separates knowledge from one-off fixesFixes that pass set-aside tests still fail on new real-world work.
The harness is worth what it costsCost per accepted result rises as the harness grows.

Our positions on three hard questions

  • Who checks the checkers? Reality does. Internal tests and reviewers are fast, cheap stand-ins for the real verdict, which comes from operational and market results: what customers did, what worked and what didn't. When a test keeps passing work that the market rejects, the test is wrong and gets fixed. The chain of checking ends in outcomes, not in another layer of reviewers.
  • Where does genuinely new knowledge come from? From people. The harness applies, refines and tests knowledge, and it can tell real knowledge from a one-off fix. Truly new explanations, such as a new strategy or a new way of seeing the business, are created by people. Whether that happens depends on culture more than on technology (Pillar 4). The harness's job is to make people's new ideas cheap to try, honest to test and easy to keep.
  • Can agents choose problems? Yes, but within bounds. An agent can spot patterns in failures, propose problems and rank them inside the brief it has been given. Deutsch argues that people are universal explainers, able in principle to understand anything that can be understood. On our reading, that is why people choose freely which problems matter and set the agents' briefs in the first place. This is a position, not a proven fact; it would be challenged if agents began to originate problems outside their brief that people judged more valuable than their own.

Still open

  • How do you retire workarounds that depend on each other? Testing them one at a time is easy; testing dozens that interact is not.
  • How quickly can market signals be connected back to a specific fix? Internal tests answer in minutes and markets in months, so the harness needs a way to link slow outcomes to the cards that produced them.

13. Conclusion

Satya Nadella has named the defining economic question of enterprise AI: who captures the value of correcting errors? His five C's are a sensible checklist for protecting that value.

But the lasting advantage isn't a walled-off store of logs, in anyone's cloud. It is an organization that can notice a consequential mistake, explain it, try a fix and test it honestly, faster than its competitors, and that gets simpler every time a better model arrives. The harness is the machinery that makes this routine. The people who choose the problems and criticize the answers are what make it work.

We don't expect either the enterprise or the model to win outright. The more likely future is a shifting division of labor in which every problem solved exposes a new and more valuable one.
