Epistemological Critique

Ads Don't Work That Way

An Ehrenberg-Bass and Popperian critique of Kevin Simler's model of advertising — why neither emotional inception nor cultural imprinting explains how a brand gets into your head, and what a universal explainer has to do with it.

Author Karl
Co-Author Shubham Mehrish
Published August 11, 2026
Subject Advertising & Epistemology

Abstract

Kevin Simler's "Ads Don't Work That Way" attacks emotional inception — the Pavlovian story that ads seed positive feelings that tip us toward a purchase — and replaces it with cultural imprinting: ads change the landscape of social meaning so that buying is a signal to peers. He is right that ads don't persuade; he is wrong that the dominant mechanism is social signaling. The buying-behavior evidence (Ehrenberg-Bass) says the operative mechanism is mental availability — a thin, individual retrieval link built mostly by usage. And the deeper error is his model of the mind: both his rejected and his accepted theories treat a person as a mechanism rather than a universal explainer. Reconciling the two, a brand identity is "a hypothesis the consumer holds"— usually a compressed retrieval cue — and a new brand breaks its cold-start deadlock precisely because people can form and test hypotheses about things they have never experienced.

1. The Source

Kevin Simler's Ads Don't Work That Way (Melting Asphalt, 2014, republished by Stripe/Works in Progress in August 2026) attacks a meme he calls emotional inception — the popular, Mad Men–flavored theory that advertising works by quietly seeding positive, Pavlovian emotional associations in our brains, which deepen over time and tip us toward a purchase. He argues this portrays us as "puppets at the mercy of every Tom, Dick, and Harry with a billboard," demands too high a burden of proof for such a "flat and passive" medium, and should be rejected.

"An ad doesn't change our individual minds so much as it changes the landscape of cultural meanings — what a product 'says' about you to your peers. Whether you drink Corona or Heineken or Budweiser 'says' something about you. But you aren't in control of that message; it just sits there, out in the world, having been imprinted on the broader culture by an ad campaign."

— Kevin Simler, "Ads Don't Work That Way"

In its place he offers a set of "honest" mechanisms (awareness, persuasion, promises, honest signaling) plus one dominant, darker one he calls cultural imprinting. Because it relies on common knowledge — everyone knowing that everyone else knows — it must be broadcast conspicuously; and once it takes root, there is no immunity. You buy not in spite of your rationality but because of it — as a utility-maximizing Homo economicus pursuing the fixed goal of peer respect.

The essay is intellectually honest in the best way: it names the enemy theory, attacks it, proposes a rival, and draws testable predictions (Superbowl audiences should scale more-than-linearly; bed sheets and gas stations should advertise less than soda; brands must stay single-faced; you can't advertise to yourself). This is the method of conjecture and refutation done in the open, and it deserves real credit. My critique splits into three movements: what the buying-behavior evidence says, what the soul says, and how the two can be reconciled.

Evidence

2. What Ehrenberg-Bass Agrees With

Byron Sharp and the Ehrenberg-Bass Institute would cheer the essay's negative half. The central thesis of How Brands Grow is that advertising rarely works by persuasion — not by argument, not by changing attitudes, not by converting the undecided. Most of what we call persuasion is, empirically, reinforcement and reminder: refreshing the memory structures that make a brand come to mind at the moment of choice. Simler's demolition of "ads argue us into buying" is squarely on the right side of the evidence.

He also lands on a real E-B observation: brands must stay consistent over decades. His "why aren't brands two-faced?" argument correctly notices that brands occupy a narrow slice of identity space for a long time. E-B would explain this through distinctive assets — color, logo, jingle, shape — whose function is easy recognition, not personality. So the single-face observation is genuine; the dispute is over why.

3. Where Ehrenberg-Bass Diverges

This is the crux. Simler's positive thesis — that ads work chiefly by cultural imprinting — is not what the buying-behavior data show. E-B's account is that ads work mainly through mental availability (and physical availability): being thought of in the buying situation. That is an individual, at-retail memory phenomenon — not primarily a social-coordination game. Four empirical generalizations cut hard against cultural imprinting:

01
Double Jeopardy & Polygamy
Bigger brands have more buyers and slightly higher loyalty, purely as a function of size. Buyers are polygamous — they hold a repertoire and switch within it (NBD-Dirichlet). This is fatal to the crisp "Corona says chill" story: consumers mostly don't hold sharply differentiated brand images.
02
Growth From Penetration
Brand growth is driven by attracting light and non-category buyers — by who thinks of you in the aisle, not by deepening any coherent identity. "Being in the consideration set" dominates "what message does my purchase send."
03
A Weak, Defensive Force
Measured advertising effects are small, cumulative, and largely defensive — keeping salience from decaying. The image of an ad "rewriting desire," which Simler mocks in the inception model, is also too strong for his model.
04
Reach Beats Bursts
E-B's media findings and recency planning favor broad, continuous presence over concentrated "common-knowledge" moments. Simler's Superbowl more-than-linearity conjecture is exactly the claim E-B would interrogate — and the evidence tends the other way.

The sharpest empirical tension is his prediction that we should not find effective branding in fragmented, personal digital media ("internet search ads and banner ads are inimical to cultural imprinting"). Yet a huge share of effective brand advertising is exactly that — personalized, fragmented, delivered individually. E-B resolves this cleanly: that media works because it builds individual mental availability, the very thing Simler's model tells you can't work.

Fair summary of the empirical side: Simler correctly demolishes the persuasion/inception model, but his replacement over-weights social signaling at the expense of the far more mundane, far better-evidenced mechanism of salience and memory.
The Soul

4. Where Karl's Popperian Soul Pushes Back

The deeper problem isn't his data — it's his model of the human mind. Both the theory he rejects (Pavlovian conditioning) and the theory he offers (rational social-signaling under common knowledge) treat a person as a mechanism: input → association → output. The first is a bell-and-food machine; the second is a utility-maximizing calculator. Neither is an account of a person — a universal explainer who conjectures, criticizes, and creates new knowledge. This is the heart of the disagreement.

He defends Homo economicus from the wrong direction

The whole essay keeps preferences "fixed and exogenous" so that ads can be said to change only the means, never the goals: "we buy because of our rationality, not in spite of it." But preferences and goals are knowledge — and knowledge is changeable, and changeable by us. The true dichotomy is not "mechanistic inception vs. rational signaling." It is mechanical vs. agentic. Simler stays firmly on the mechanical side of that line in both his rejected and his accepted model.

He refutes a theory partly on dignity grounds

A Popperian cannot object that emotional inception "does us a disservice" or "portrays us as less rational than we are." Those are aesthetic and moral complaints, not refutations. A theory's truth does not depend on how flattering it is to us. To his credit, Simler also offers falsifiable predictions — that part is genuinely good. 

"No immunity" is fatalism, not truth

His conclusion — once we've seen the ad and know our peers have, it is rationally self-interested to comply, so there is no escape — is a deterministic claim dressed as insight. From the soul: problems are inevitable but problems are solvable by creating new knowledge. Because we are universal explainers, no ad can determine us. The remedy is not the weak "think critically" vigilance he rightly mocks, but creative construction: counter-culture, new institutions, critique, communities of taste that change the landscape he declares fixed. "No immunity" reads a permanent barrier into what is merely a current lack of knowledge.

His favorite example betrays him

He praises the subway sugary-drink PSA for working via peer pressure. But that is precisely the mechanism the philosophy distrusts: changing behavior by conformity and social shame rather than by better explanation. "Sugary drinks raise metabolic risk" is a testable claim a person could evaluate on its merits; "your friends will judge you for drinking this" is social coercion. Peer-pressure advertising treats people as herd animals to be managed — obedience-based social engineering. A person is persuaded by reasons, not cowed into signaling compliance.

Brands and good explanations

The Popperian soul actually supports Simler's single-facedness argument, but with better reasoning than "social-signal trust." A brand identity is a hypothesis the consumer holds — a fallible, predictive, revisable theory about what the brand will deliver and stand for, not a fixed impression stamped into the mind. And a good hypothesis, like a good explanation, is hard to vary while still being that brand. Chaotic, jumpy branding is a bad explanation: you cannot know what it stands for. That is consistency-as-explanatory-quality, not consistency-as-social-coordination. This claim deserves one important refinement — that it must survive contact with E-B's mental availability — which I turn to next.

Reconciliation

5. The Hypothesis, Adjusted for Mental Availability

Calling a brand identity "a hypothesis the consumer holds" initially sounds richer and more deliberate than the buying data allow. E-B's mental availability is the corrective: the operative structure for most buying is not a meaty evaluative theory but a thin retrieval link between the brand and the category entry points — the occasions and needs that trigger purchase. Those cannot both be the whole story, so the hypothesis needs adjusting in four ways.

First, bifurcate it

A brand identity is a hypothesis the consumer holds, but it exists in two states, and E-B is right that the thinner one does most of the work:

Full-Strength Hypothesis Compressed Retrieval Cue
What it is A deliberate, predictive, evaluative theory — including any social prediction ("bringing Corona reads as chill"). E-B's mental availability: a cached, low-involvement link ("when I think 'barbecue beer' → Corona is a candidate").
Where it matters First trial of an unfamiliar brand; high-stakes or high-involvement choices (a car, insurance); identity-loaded conspicuous categories. The bulk of habitual, repertoire-based purchasing — the brand simply surfaces at the buying moment.
Mental load Conscious, deliberate conjecture. Low involvement, near-automatic.
Advertising's job Prompt the conjecture; supply evidence and risk-reduction. Refresh the link; a weak reminder that maintains an existing structure.

Second, the cue is the hypothesis operationalized, not its refutation

A link like "barbecue beer → Corona" is itself a stored prediction — "this brand is a viable option for this occasion" — the crystallized residue of past conjectures that were confirmed by exposure and, above all, by usage. This cuts for agency, not against it: mental availability is built and refreshed by category entry points, broad reach, and chiefly by actual experience with the product — not by the ad. The ad is a weak reminder that reactivates a cue; the substance of what the consumer believes is mostly earned through their own encounters with the brand. So the ad is an even smaller input than Simler's cultural-imprinting story implies, which makes the "no immunity" rebuttal stronger, not weaker.

Third, concede the hypothesis is usually thin

The good-explanation argument and the "Corona signals chill" example both presuppose a fairly meaty, differentiated image. E-B says that for most brands it isn't there — small brands aren't "more differentiated," they're just smaller (double jeopardy). The essay should not lean on identity richness. But the good-explanation property survives in retrievability form: E-B's distinctive assets are consistency-for-recognition — a brand with a stable color, logo, and jingle is unambiguously retrieved and categorized, which is exactly the mental-availability analog of "hard to vary." Consistency = a clean, reliable retrieval link. The two accounts unify right here.

Fourth, keep the levels straight

E-B's laws — double jeopardy, repertoire buying, salience — are population-level regularities: aggregate, probabilistic, law-like. The hypothesis view is individual and explanatory. These are not competitors but different levels of description, like statistical mechanics versus the trajectories of the molecules. Aggregate regularities can emerge from a population of fallible, conjecturing, sometimes-overriding agents; you don't need individuals to be calculators for the population to obey NBD-Dirichlet. So both can be held at once: mental availability is the law that governs the crowd; the hypothesis is what each person in the crowd is doing.

A final guard against overcorrecting: a retrieval cue raises the probability of consideration; it does not determine the choice. The capacity to stop, think, and override the default cue — to discount it or reject the brand — remains, and that is precisely the universal explainer. Mental availability describes the prior that shapes consideration; it does not eliminate the agent.

Lifecycle

6. Cold Start: How a New Brand Gets Off the Ground

The framework above silently assumes every brand is already established. But consider a brand with zero usage, zero memory structures, and zero retrieval links. E-B says availability is built by usage — which makes a new brand look like a chicken-and-egg deadlock: no usage → no availability → no sales → no usage. How does a brand with no history ever launch?

The resolution is the very property the soul insists on: a consumer can form a hypothesis without personal experience. The apparent paradox rests on a false premise — that a brand image must be built from usage. But knowledge doesn't begin from a blank slate of experience; it begins from expectations and conjectures that then get tested. A person meeting a brand for the first time doesn't wait for evidence to accumulate; they build a fallible theory from whatever else is available and correct it on first use. So a new brand doesn't wait for a usage history — it borrows credibility and invites the consumer to conjecture. The cold-start toolkit is exactly the set of things that substitute for missing personal experience:

01
Category Analogy
"I've liked craft lagers before; this is a craft lager." The consumer generalizes from the category to the unknown brand.
02
House Reputation
Disney launching a streamer inherits trust from Disney. A known parent manufactures instant hypothesis-credibility.
03
Honest Signaling
A large launch spend is itself information: "we're committed, we're here to stay, we'll support this." It substitutes for history the brand doesn't yet have.
04
Third-Party Proof
Reviews, ratings, awards, certifications. Evidence the consumer can weigh without buying.
05
Risk-Reduction
Free samples, trial packs, money-back guarantees, free tiers, return policies. This pays the consumer to run the experiment: the cheaper the downside of a wrong hypothesis, the more willing a rational person is to form one.

The first trial is the bottleneck, and it is where the full-strength hypothesis (not the compressed cue) does the work. This turns the two states of Section 5 into a lifecycle rather than merely two coexisting options. An established brand runs on the thin retrieval cue — low involvement, habitual purchase, advertising as a weak reminder. A new brand has no cue to run on, so it must prompt a deliberate, predictive theory ("is this worth trying?") to justify a first trial; that trial becomes usage; usage builds the memory structure; and the structure crystallizes into the compressed cue. Cold start inverts the steady-state dynamic: the launch forces a conscious conjecture, the steady state runs on an unconscious cue.

E-B adds the hard part: a new brand must manufacture the availability it cannot yet maintain, and the only way is reach, distribution, and distinctive assets from day one — buying penetration by getting into the consideration set of as many category buyers as possible. This quietly refines E-B's "advertising is a weak, defensive force": that is true for established brands; at launch, advertising and distribution are creating the structure rather than maintaining it.

The cold start does not end at the first sale — it ends at the first use, when the consumer tests the borrowed hypothesis against the product. If the product refutes it, the brand dies (the conjecture is falsified); if it confirms, the hypothesis hardens into a cue and the brand transitions to the availability regime. This is why most new brands fail and why advertising alone cannot save a bad product: the marketer proposes a hypothesis, but the consumer is the one who tests it. A brand is an argument to a critic, not a stimulus to a reflex.

The payoff is that Simler's "honest" mechanisms and E-B's availability turn out to describe different phases of the lifecycle, not competing theories of the same moment. Cold start is hypothesis-driven — where Simler's taxonomy is more right. Steady state is availability-driven — where E-B is more right. The relative weights shift over a brand's life: a new brand lives on conjecture; an established one lives on memory.

Conclusion

7. A Synthesis Both an "Ehrenbergian" and a Popperian Could Sign

Advertising is real but weak. It works by building and refreshing what people remember and believe, and people are always free to critique, reject, and create better memes in response. That is both empirically truer than cultural imprinting and morally truer than "no immunity."

The essay is a model of conjecture and refutation in the informal sphere — naming the enemy theory, attacking it, proposing a rival, offering discriminating tests. That is the correct epistemic method, and it deserves real credit. But it fails at two levels:

The Evidence Says
Verdict
He is right that ads don't persuade; wrong that the dominant mechanism is cultural signaling. The measured mechanism is mental availability — salience and memory at the point of purchase, mostly defensive, mostly individual.
The Soul Says
Verdict
He swaps one mechanistic account of the mind for another. A person is neither a conditioned dog nor a signaling calculator, but an open-ended explainer. And his "no immunity" is the philosophy's cardinal sin — anti-human pessimism, mistaking a solvable problem for a permanent prison.

The two critiques are not in tension once the level distinction is kept: mental availability is how the population of buyers behaves; the hypothesis is what each individual is doing beneath the aggregate. The soul supplies the correct model of the agent; E-B supplies the correct model of the crowd.

The enemy was never really the ad. The enemy is any theory — inception, imprinting, or a naive reading of salience — that reduces a person to something that can be run, rather than someone who can think. Ads are memes seeking hosts; a person is not a passive host but a critic. That is the one mechanism of advertising every model, including Simler's, leaves out.
