David Deutsch @DavidDeutschOxf
🗓️ Published on November 10, 2026

"The scattershot technique is telling so many lies, so fast in parallel that no one can correct them within anyone's attention span. But "legitimacy…by all available means including armed struggle" is the serial-lying version: makes refuting one first seem to endorse the others."

🔑 Key Takeaway

Deutsch names two structurally different ways of lying about which a good explanation must be precise. The 'scattershot technique' overwhelms correction by volume: so many false claims arrive in parallel that each individual refutation is obsolete before it lands within anyone's attention span. The 'serial' version inverts the failure mode — a chain of equivocal statements ('legitimacy by all available means including armed struggle') is ordered so that refuting the first step seems to endorse whatever comes next, tying the correction itself into the misleading chain. The analytical payoff is that these demand different responses: one needs prioritisation and patience, the other needs the ambiguity exposed and each link of the chain separated before it can be refuted. It is the same discipline the feed keeps returning to — effective criticism depends on making the target crisp enough to be tested.

Margaret Thatcher @realmrsthatcher
🗓️ Published on November 10, 2026

"No one would deny that in every age and in every society there are features of which we should be ashamed, but can we honestly say that the system built up on private enterprise and freedom of choice has not produced an immense change for the better in the lot of all our people?"

🔑 Key Takeaway

Thatcher refuses the all-or-nothing judgement that nicer-sounding systems demand. The premise concedes that no society, including a free one, is without shameful features — an honest acknowledgment that pre-empts the usual tu quoque. But the substantive question is comparative and empirical, not ideal: has a system of private enterprise and freedom of choice actually produced 'an immense change for the better in the lot of all our people'? Framing it as a question ('can we honestly say... not') keeps it testable: the claim is not that such a system is perfect, but that it has demonstrably raised the material lot of ordinary people, and that this measured improvement is the relevant ground for judging it. This is the feed's epistemology in economics form — judging institutions by their real, error-corrected consequences for individuals rather than by their stated ideals.

David Deutsch @DavidDeutschOxf
🗓️ Published on November 9, 2026

"It's the anniversary of Reichspogromnacht. The current pogrom is not (yet) as bad. Nevertheless, it's a tragedy for the Anglosphere that it is participating this time."

🔑 Key Takeaway

Deutsch anchors a contemporary warning in the concrete anniversary of Kristallnacht. The measured comparison — 'not (yet) as bad' — is itself a model of disciplined judgement: it neither inflates the present into a repeat of the Holocaust nor dismisses it, but locates it on the same continuum of anti-Jewish violence. The sharper point is the shift in who is acting: in 1938 the perpetrators and spectators were elsewhere, whereas now 'the Anglosphere' — the civilisation whose institutions the victims' descendants built — is itself a participant, not a bystander. The epistemic-moral load is that civilisational self-government makes one responsible not only for what one does but for what one tolerates, and that the capacity to forestall the worse outcome ('not yet as bad') depends on not treating the less-bad stage as acceptable.

Brett Hall (ToK Teacher) @ToKTeacher
🗓️ Published on November 9, 2026

"@ContrarianJolly @IAI_TV "The brain is a computer". Right ✅ By the way it's only "fundamentally" different in the sense that a "fundamentally different" kind of software (the mind) is running on it. But in principle all its operations could be done by a silicon computer. Or Turing machine, etc."

🔑 Key Takeaway

Hall sharpens the computationalist claim against the objection that brains are 'fundamentally different' from computers. His move is to relocate the supposed difference: it does not lie in the physical substrate or the hardware, where the Church-Turing-Deutsch principle already covers every physical system as a computer, but in the software — the mind — that runs on it. A silicon computer or Turing machine could in principle perform every operation a brain performs; nothing about the physics of the brain is unreproducible by a universal machine. So anyone who insists the brain is fundamentally different owes an account of why the laws of physics that explain every other material system should fail for brains. As with his earlier exchange, the force of the argument is its falsifiability — 'propose new physics' — rather than an appeal to intuition or the ineffability of consciousness.

Naval Ravikant @naval
🗓️ Published on November 6, 2026

"Not money, not time - but attention."

🔑 Key Takeaway

Naval compresses a universal principle into a single sentence: the scarce resource that ultimately constraints every human undertaking is neither money nor time but attention. Money can be earned and time is abundant relative to the real bottleneck — the focused directed awareness that must be spent to create anything, understand anything, or influence anyone. The claim generalises Popperian epistemology into an economics of mind: knowledge creation is attention-intensive, and because attention cannot be stored or delegated without loss, it is the one input every creative process depends on but can never fully outsource. Read as a guide to action it reorders priorities: what you give your attention to is what you are, and the most valuable thing you cannot buy — or recover once scattered — is the concentrated attention of another person's (or your own) mind.

Naval Ravikant @naval
🗓️ Published on November 6, 2026

"The ideal passive portfolio is a broad collection of highly asymmetric long-term bets."

🔑 Key Takeaway

A compressed theory of how to hold knowledge under uncertainty. Naval's 'ideal passive portfolio' is a broad collection of highly asymmetric long-term bets — the point is to be wrong cheaply and right expensively. Spreading across many positions acknowledges that no single conjecture about the future is reliable, while weighting for asymmetry and long horizons ensures that the bets that pay off (the ones where you are right on the upside) dominate the many that fail. This is conjecture-and-refutation applied to capital: you cannot know in advance which thesis is correct, so you hold many, keep each failure cheap, and give the winners room to compound. It fits the feed's epistemology because it treats uncertainty not as an obstacle to be eliminated but as the standing condition of action, best handled by a structure that profits from the few correct conjectures while surviving the many refuted ones.

Naval Ravikant @naval
🗓️ Published on November 4, 2026

"Greed can be satisfied. Envy, cannot."

🔑 Key Takeaway

A precise distinction between two motives with opposite epistemic dynamics. Greed is a desire for more of something definable, and because its object is a definite scarce good it can in principle be satisfied — you can reach the point where you have enough. Envy, by contrast, is a desire not for a good but for a relative position — to have more than, or to deprive, another — and since relative position can always be re-shifted, satisfaction recedes forever. The line matters for the feed because it distinguishes a progressive orientation (wanting a better state of the world, achievable through problem-solving and value-creation) from a zero-sum one (wanting to be ahead, achievable only by tearing others down). Greed, disciplined, can fuel creation; envy can only ever demand destruction. It is a compact statement of why an open society rewards the first and is poisoned by the second.
