"Just to unpack: 1. Creativity cannot be automated. If we “automate” creativity we *have* creativity. Automation, properly considered, is the thing that is a reliable repetition of *the same processes* over and over, or processes already known turned over the mindless..."

🔑 Key Takeaway

Hall unpacks a distinction that cuts to the core of the AGI debate: automation, properly understood, is the reliable repetition of the same known processes, mindlessly re-run. Creativity cannot be automated in that sense, because if you “automate” creativity you have thereby produced creativity — you have not removed it but achieved it. The companion claim completes the picture: creations cannot be automations, and minds cannot be simulations. This is a resolution of the apparent tension in the current LLM moment: a program that reliably reproduces known procedures is automation, however fluent it looks, while genuine creativity — the production of new explanatory knowledge — is precisely what resists reduction to a fixed, repeatable process. Read through the error-correction lens, the point is that automation is a closed loop (it re-runs what is already known), whereas creative understanding is an open one (it modifies its own methods and aims). Conflating the two is the deepest category error in modern AI commentary, and Hall’s crisp one-line formulation — creations cannot be automations, minds cannot be simulations — is a good, hard-to-vary explanation worth keeping in mind whenever someone claims a machine has crossed into genuine understanding.

"@kssreeram A mind is, physically, infinitely many quantum fungible instances anyways. If one adds to this: what if we copy a mind and run it such that it never diverges? Well it’s just another fungible instance. We can regard both copies as a single person, still. *Differentiation* makes..."

🔑 Key Takeaway

Hall makes a striking point about the metaphysics of mind and identity by appealing to physics. A mind, physically, is not a unique object but one of infinitely many quantum-fungible instances of a configuration; what kind of thing it is is shared, not individuated. If you were to copy a mind and run it so that it never diverges, it would simply be another fungible instance — still, in an important sense, the same person, because identity here is about the explanatory structure, not the particular substrate or the particular copy. What makes two minds into two distinct persons, rather than two interchangeable tokens of one person, is *differentiation*: the fact that they must occupy different space and receive different inputs, so that over time they accumulate distinct histories. This reverses a common intuition — that copies are automatically separate persons — by locating personhood in the ongoing process of differential history rather than in the physical token. It is a deeply Popperian move: it refuses a naive substrate-identity and replaces it with an explanatory account of what actually does the individuating. The argument is subtle, and it matters for the coming age of digital minds, because it says the boundary between “me” and “another copy of me” is drawn by divergence itself — not by the hardware.

🔑 Key Takeaway

Hall presses the criterion that grounds the entire personhood discussion: if a universal capacity to create explanatory knowledge is not what uniquely distinguishes persons — humans, AGI, alien intelligences alike — then what does? The word he stresses is explanatory: he is careful to note that this is different from merely generating new knowledge, since even mindless biological evolution generates new knowledge by variation and selection. What people do is create explanatory knowledge, and they are universal in that capacity — able, in principle, to understand anything that can be understood. This is the Deutschian thesis that persons are universal explainers. The rhetorical structure of the post matters: he is not merely asserting a definition but issuing a challenge. If you reject the criterion, he asks, you must supply a better one — and articulate what, then, makes persons unique at all. The question is a classic error-correction device: force the interlocutor to commit to a conjecture, then expose it to criticism. It keeps the debate honest by refusing to let personhood rest on an unexamined, automatic property.

David Deutsch @DavidDeutschOxf
🗓️ Published on November 22, 2026

"@kevinschawinski You're expecting them to have explanatory creativity in regard to maths and physics but not morality, or something? https://t.co/lCX4TnPUsw"

🔑 Key Takeaway

Deutsch lands a sharp reductio against a familiar double standard in AI commentary. The objection being addressed runs roughly: machines can exhibit creativity in mathematics and physics, but morality is somehow off-limits — a realm where explanatory creativity is either impossible or inappropriate for a machine. Deutsch’s reply exposes the inconsistency: if you grant that an entity has genuine explanatory creativity in maths and physics, why would you arbitrarily deny that it could apply the same creative, explanatory capacity to morality? The challenge works because morality, on a Popperian-Deutschian account, is not a mysteriously different kind of knowledge. It is itself the output of the same universal creative process — conjecture, criticism, and error-elimination applied to problems of how to live. If the capacity for explanatory creativity is real and general, it must range over moral problems too, however uncomfortable that conclusion is. The post is a model of intellectual honesty: it refuses a comfortable, morally charged carve-out and insists that a genuinely universal capacity stays universal, or else it was never genuinely universal at all.

David Deutsch @DavidDeutschOxf
🗓️ Published on November 21, 2026

"@jwesamuel @DarlaSkyee If the LLM has explanatory creativity, it hasn't *demonstrated* it. But yes, either it has it or this proof, contrary to everyone's expectation including mine, didn't require any. Or it used its human collaborator's. Or other possibilities. We'll have to wait and see."

🔑 Key Takeaway

Deutsch maintains a characteristically precise epistemic stance about whether a large language model possesses explanatory creativity. His claim is narrowly and carefully negative: if the LLM has explanatory creativity, it has not *demonstrated* it. He then lists the live possibilities: either the machine genuinely has the capacity, or the proof in question did not actually require it (contrary to everyone’s expectation, including his own), or the model relied on its human collaborator’s creativity, or some other explanation holds. The striking thing is the refusal to settle the question prematurely. This is Popperian inquiry at its most disciplined: enumerate the live, mutually incompatible conjectures, admit ignorance about which is true, and commit to finding out by further evidence rather than by preference or wishful thinking. It also models humility — Deutsch explicitly includes the possibility that his own prior expectations were wrong. The takeaway is that the boundary between fluent pattern-completion and genuine creative explanation is an empirical question to be settled by careful demonstration, not by rhetoric about capabilities.

Naval Ravikant @naval
🗓️ Published on November 21, 2026

"Is there a thinker behind the thought? Is there a doer behind the deed? Is there an observer behind the observation?"

🔑 Key Takeaway

Naval compresses a deep philosophical provocation into three symmetrical questions, and the answer he moves toward is 'no' — there is no thinker behind the thought, no doer behind the deed, no observer behind the observation. The point is the rejection of a homunculus, an extra hidden agent that would itself need explaining and would launch an infinite regress. Thought just is the process that produces the appearance of a thinker; the self is not a further thing doing the thinking but is constituted by the activity itself. Read through the error-correction lens, the insight is liberating rather than nihilistic: our mistaken pictures of an inner controller are the residue of bad explanations about mind, and dissolving them clears the way for better theories of how understanding, agency, and experience actually arise from the same substrate that explains everything else.

🗓️ Published on November 17, 2026

"He's actually bull posting - it's just that the coin is encrypted."

🔑 Key Takeaway

Khemani's one-liner is a reflexive joke about the nature of crypto commentary, and it carries a genuinely Popperian sting. The surface reading — that a confident market forecast is just performative 'bull posting,' made indistinguishable by the obscurity of the asset — is a wry admission that much of the space is conjecture dressed as certainty. But the deeper point is about the impossibility of certifying explanations in advance: whether a claim about an encrypted coin is signal or noise can never be settled by the posture of the poster, only by exposure to real-world criticism and refutation, which is exactly what a competitive, liquid market provides. Optimism survives the joke because it is not confidence in any particular call but confidence in the error-correction process itself — someone, somewhere is always betting, testing, and being corrected, and it is that machinery, not any individual's bravado, that does the epistemic work.
