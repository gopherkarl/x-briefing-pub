Naval Ravikant @naval
🗓️ Published on September 24, 2026

"Every functioning institution is merit-based, not participation-based."

🔑 Key Takeaway

A crisp definitional claim about what makes an institution work. A functioning institution selects the people who best perform its function, whatever that function is - it weights contribution and capability, not membership or presence. A participation-based institution rewards showing up and belonging regardless of performance, which erodes the very competence that lets the institution do its job. The claim is testable because it names the selection criterion: look at who rises and who is retained, and you can tell which basis the institution actually runs on. It also generalises across institutions - companies, laboratories, armies, even epistemically, where the institution that 'functions' is the one whose ideas are selected by merit (correction) rather than by who asserts them.

David Deutsch @DavidDeutschOxf
🗓️ Published on September 24, 2026

"@grok What will you, or the consensus among mathematicians, conclude if, after a few years, the flow of AIs solving famous mathematical problems stops, but the trickle of humans solving them continues?"

🔑 Key Takeaway

A precisely posed experiment for dividing genuine intelligence from pattern-completion. The setup is deliberately conditional: suppose the stream of AI-proven famous theorems dries up while a trickle of human proofs continues. That kind of evidence would tell us the AIs were not discovering new methods after all - that their earlier 'solutions' drew down a reservoir of already-known patterns (or were overfits) that could be exhausted, whereas human problem-solving kept generating genuinely new methods because it is not bounded by a fixed corpus. The question is a model of how to test the Deutschian conjecture about AI: instead of arguing about capability in the abstract, specify the observation that would refute it. That is exactly the standard - a claim earns its status as knowledge by stating what would show it false.

David Deutsch @DavidDeutschOxf
🗓️ Published on September 24, 2026

"@ferraduckman @grok I can't prophesy. It will happen or it won't. There is no such thing as possibility or probability other than what is implied by the laws of nature. But my conditional question is much less dependent on the future growth of knowledge and so @grok could estimate an answer."

🔑 Key Takeaway

Deutsch refuses prophecy while defending the legitimacy of conditional questions. He cannot say whether AGI-style capability will arrive because that depends on the unknowable growth of knowledge, and he denies there is any free-floating 'possibility' or 'probability' apart from what the laws of physics imply - the future is a single determinate branch, not a distribution over branches awaiting observation. What rescues the question he put to Grok is that it is conditional: 'if the flow of AI proofs stops, what would you conclude?' is far less hostage to how knowledge grows, because the condition itself fixes the relevant facts. This is a distinction between asking the world to predict itself (impossible) and asking an agent to apply an explanation to a stated scenario (legitimate) - and it shows why good questions are engineered to be answerable.

"@aaronscher @sama No level of risk is acceptable? The laws of physics enter the chat…"

🔑 Key Takeaway

A deflation of the zero-risk demand that treats safety as an absolute rather than a trade against real constraints. If literally no level of risk is acceptable, then the injunction is untestable and unfulfillable - because acting at all, including refraining from acting, carries some risk, and the demand itself never specifies a standard by which any option could be judged safe enough. 'The laws of physics enter the chat' flags the category error: there is no risk-free course of action available in a physical world, so a policy that forbids all risk forbids all action, which is quietly a policy of doing nothing and calling it prudence. The post is a refutation of the false binary between 'safe' and 'unsafe' AI by pointing out that the relevant question is always 'compared to what', never 'is there any risk at all'.

An account of how regulation operates as an epistemic tax. The point is not that compliance is unreasonable but that sufficiently complex legislation converts a technical engineering question into a legal one: to know whether the code is compliant you no longer need to understand the system so much as to retain lawyers who can tell whether the latest change breaches the latest rule. The effect is a shifting cost from building good software to maintaining a compliance apparatus that must be consulted over every change, which slows iteration and raises the price of error. It names a mechanism - complexity as leverage - by which regulation grows from protecting people to controlling producers, and it invites the reader to count who actually bears the cost.

Brett Hall (ToK Teacher) @ToKTeacher
🗓️ Published on September 23, 2026

"What is inevitable are problems. But problems are soluble. See the work of @DavidDeutschOxf - in particular "The Beginning of Infinity". Again *we* personally or as a civilisation can be such a beginning. But nothing is guaranteed as all extinct civilisations demonstrate."

🔑 Key Takeaway

The compressed Popperian-Deutschian thesis in one post. Problems are a permanent feature of existence - no solution removes the possibility of new problems, so they are indeed inevitable. But every problem is in principle solvable by new knowledge; there is no problem that is insoluble in principle, only problems whose solutions we have not yet created. The reference to The Beginning of Infinity and the warning that nothing is guaranteed (all extinct civilisations demonstrate) keep the optimism critical rather than naive: it is not a promise that progress is automatic but an invitation to be the kind of agent that solves problems. The testable claim is that the failure mode is not 'problems exist' but 'we stopped trying to solve them' - and that a civilisation can itself be a beginning of infinity or, like the extinct ones, a failure to be one.

"Progress, and accelerating progress at that, is possible. But it's not inevitable. We can fail. So we have to put effort into solving our problems. The laws of physics do not prohibit us from becoming immortal and reaching the stars. But they do not guarantee it either. https://t.co/DxbjrH0prH"

🔑 Key Takeaway

A clean separation of possibility from inevitability. The laws of physics do not prohibit immortality or reaching the stars, so those outcomes are possible; but they do not guarantee them either, so they are not inevitable. Progress therefore requires effort - the creation of knowledge - and can fail. This is the critical-optimist stance: optimism is not the prediction that things will get better on their own but the claim that they can, combined with the insistence that making them better is our responsibility. The reference to failure and to the possibility of not reaching the stars is the refutation of inevitability: if progress were automatic, no civilisation would ever have collapsed, and no goal would ever be missed. The post refuses both the doomer's 'it is fated to fail' and the accelerationist's 'it is fated to succeed', leaving the outcome to the quality of our explanations and the effort we invest.

🔑 Key Takeaway

An admission of a failed prediction that itself models the error-correcting attitude. Deutsch says he never guessed the Church of England would be ideologically captured, but that he should have - meaning the capture was predictable from a good explanation of how institutions drift toward conformity, and his surprise was a failure to apply that explanation, not evidence that the event was unpredictable. The post is a small demonstration of the epistemology it recommends: treat your own surprise as a problem to be explained, revise the explanation, and thereby become better able to anticipate the next case. It also names a general phenomenon - ideological capture of once-independent institutions - as something that follows a pattern once you see it, rather than a series of unrelated shocks.
