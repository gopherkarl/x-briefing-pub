Arjun Khemani @arjunkhemani
🗓️ Published on November 5, 2026

"As Popper said, “We may become the makers of our fate when we have ceased to pose as its prophets.” Doom becomes inevitable only when we cease to believe we can overcome it."

🔑 Key Takeaway

A Popperian rebuke to fatalism, and the feed's core creed stated in one post. 'Posing as its prophets' is the error: treating the future as a fixed script that we can only predict or await. The moment we stop prophesying doom and instead treat the future as something to be made rather than foretold, we become the agents of our own fate. The reason doom becomes inevitable only when we cease to believe we can overcome it is that belief here is not sentiment but a design decision: a society that sees its problems as solvable will generate the explanations and institutions that solve them, whereas a society convinced of its decline stops trying and thereby manufactures the very outcome it predicts. This is optimism not as mood but as a testable claim about cause and effect — that our expectations shape our capability, and prophecy is what we make when we forget we are the makers.

"Money you’ve spent your entire life working hard for isn’t yours the moment you deposit it in a bank. Moreover, the money itself can be created out of thin air. You can protest in the streets and hope your voice gets heard, or you can simply exit the system for a superior alternative."

🔑 Key Takeaway

An extension of his week-long theme that monetary freedom is a property of institutional structure, not of labels. The claim has two prongs. First, ownership is not a nominal fact but a question of who can decide how your assets are used: deposit money in a bank and the institution (and ultimately the state that regulates it) acquires the effective discretion over it, which is why he insists one's 'own' money is not one's own the moment it is deposited. Second, money can be created out of thin air — the supply itself is a policy decision, so the value of savings depends on whoever controls that supply. Against that, he poses two responses to perceived arbitrariness: ineffectual protest (hoping one's voice is heard) versus exit (moving to a superior alternative). On the feed's terms, exit is the agency-respecting path — using capability to move to a better system rather than petitioning the current one — while it simultaneously reframes the demand for sound money as a civilisational, not merely financial, project.

"@raisedadust @PhysInHistory Whoever says it, whether biologist, physicist, plumber or anyone else claiming humans cannot, even in principle, understand *some given thing* in the world: they’re wrong. Many “scientifically minded” people think like religious people on this topic"

🔑 Key Takeaway

A statement of the universality of human understanding — the claim that human beings are universal explainers, able in principle to understand anything that is understandable. ToKTeacher generalises the point beyond any one specialism: whether a biologist, physicist, plumber or layperson declares that humans can never understand some given thing, they are wrong, because the claim is not about a shortage of effort or tools but about an alleged limit to reason itself. The sharper observation is that many 'scientifically minded' people argue this way, and in doing so think like religious people: they treat some domain as sacrosanct and beyond explanation, which is precisely to impose a mystery where a problem — and hence a solvable one — actually stands. Naming this as a category error is an act of error-correction, because it dissolves the illusion that there is a class of things whose nature is 'forever unknowable' rather than merely unexplained so far.

"@NimeeshPatel @joshxhowie It's not as severe in Britain as in other European countries, though yes, still worsening. But there is strong resistance to it, which is unprecedented. We're in uncharted territory."

🔑 Key Takeaway

A characteristic Deutsch refusal to read a trend as settled, paired with a note of epistemic uncertainty. The subject (a decline he treats as real but 'not as severe in Britain as in other European countries') is still worsening, yet he flags 'strong resistance to it, which is unprecedented' — that is, the adverse trend is not one-way but meets organised counter-pressure. The crucial phrase is 'we're in uncharted territory': Deutsch declines to extrapolate a doom from current direction, because knowledge creation and contingency mean no trajectory is fixed and the presence of unprecedented resistance is itself a genuine novelty whose outcome cannot be read off the past. On the feed's terms this is optimism withheld from prophecy — he refuses to predict either victory or defeat, insisting instead that honest uncertainty is the correct stance when the mechanisms are still in flux and the outcome is genuinely open.

🔑 Key Takeaway

A partisan charge that is also a claim about policy mechanism. The accusation is that the desired arrangement of Europe — subsidies, socialist restrictions, protectionism — is not a neutral institutional preference but a vehicle for running (or ruining) the country, because each element transfers decision-making power from individuals and markets to bureaucrats and the state. That is the link between continental design and domestic governance: surrender economic self-determination to a protectionist, subsidy-driven supranational order and you have conceded the very choices that sovereignty was supposed to protect. The rhetorical question ('to run—or is it to ruin') forces the reader to evaluate intent against consequence. On the feed's terms, Thatcher's institutional understanding is recognisable: the test of an arrangement is whether it preserves the capacity of free agents to act, and a Europe built on subsidies and protectionism fails that test because it makes the state, not the citizen, the locus of economic power.

He endorses AI assistance in identifying relevant precedent for both sides — an 'excellent advance' because it widens and equalises access to the explanatory record — but insists the judges themselves must check it. On the feed's terms this is the right division of labour: machines amplify and audit the search for knowledge, while human judges retain the responsibility for the judgement that decides between explanations. It models the general principle that progress comes not from trusting any single authority, human or algorithmic, but from building systems where every step is itself subject to scrutiny and correction.

David Deutsch @DavidDeutschOxf
🗓️ Published on November 4, 2026

"@MrLewisYay @ilovebach59 Chamberlain, and those who voted for him, were not enemies of Britain. Germany was."

🔑 Key Takeaway

A sharp correction of a confusion between error and enmity. Chamberlain and those who voted for him pursued a policy — appeasement of Germany — that Deutsch plainly judges a disaster, yet they were not enemies of Britain; they were people who held a wrong explanation of the situation. The enemy was Germany, the actual agent with hostile intentions. On the feed's terms this distinction matters because it determines the correct response to error: if one treats those who made bad decisions as enemies, one responds with punishment and contempt, which destroys the very mechanism of error-correction (people revising their views); if one treats them as mistaken, one responds with argument and the revision of explanations. The deeper point is a refusal to collate honest-but-wrong men with the genuinely malevolent, and a reminder that the test of a theory is the correctness of its causal attribution — misidentifying the enemy is how errors compound rather than correct.
