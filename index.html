"Strong liability enforcement could be helpful in the AI debate. If your agent swarm goes rogue, you're liable. If your weakly protected model gets jailbroken, you're liable. If you serve a weakly protected OSS model, you're liable."

🔑 Key Takeaway

Liability converts an abstract risk debate into a concrete incentive mechanism without central control. The proposal is not a new regulator but a new assignment of cost: whoever deploys the system carries responsibility for its failure, and that changes the calculus at the margin — labs and hosts have a standing reason to harden exactly the weak spots that jailbreaks and rogue agents exploit. It is an elegant instance of solving by incentive rather than by command, and it has the epistemic virtue of being testable: whether it improves safety is a claim future incidents can confirm or refute.

"The most important question is whether AI will be Godlike, Genius, or Jagged."

🔑 Key Takeaway

The framing compresses a real fork. 'Godlike' is an unbounded universal explainer that makes most human limits moot; 'Genius' is extreme but bounded capability; 'Jagged' describes the actual observed profile of current systems — superhuman in some patches, subhuman in others, with the jaggedness itself largely unpredictable from the outside. The taxonomy matters because it determines the right stance: worship, deference, or careful case-by-case empirical testing. The honest answer to the question is that we do not yet know, and the reason is epistemological — a system's reach cannot be read off its architecture, only probed.

"@DivinelyDesined Almost all of science consists of things we cannot observe. We explain what we do observe in terms of what is unobserved. Unobserved dinosaurs in terms of observed fossils. Unobserved big bang in terms of observed galaxy red shifts and CMB. Unobserved iron core of the Earth in"

🔑 Key Takeaway

This is the explanatory-realist core of science stated plainly: observation is not the content of science but the evidence for it. The entities that explain — dinosaurs, the big bang, the Earth's core — are precisely the ones that cannot be observed directly; they earn their place by being part of a good explanation of what can be. This inverts the naive empiricist picture that science is 'what you can see.' What makes the unobserved entities trustworthy is not that we met them but that the explanations containing them are hard to vary and account for the evidence — which is why inference to unobservables is not a weakness of science but its defining strength.

""…there is nothing stopping the mathematics community from awarding status, pay, and promotions to people who "fill in the important blanks in math understanding," even if an AI already has proven or disproven the underlying theorems.""

🔑 Key Takeaway

The institutional point is subtle: a community's rewards reflect what its members value, and nothing forces them to value only 'first proof.' If filling explanatory gaps and deepening understanding is treated as the real contribution — the thing that gets status, pay and promotion — then AI proving or disproving theorems need not displace human mathematicians at all; it frees them to do the higher-order work of explanation. Whether that happens is a choice about incentives, not a technological inevitability. The general lesson is that the value of an activity is set by the community that rewards it, and that is a matter of culture, which can be changed.

David Deutsch @DavidDeutschOxf
🗓️ Published on September 12, 2026

"I just fixed my dishwasher with the help of ChatGPT. A trivial task. I had been about to order a new one. So this software has increased the country's real wealth yet decreased the measured GDP. The main economic indicator is structurally incapable of registering the thing that"

🔑 Key Takeaway

The GDP paradox is a category error exposed in miniature. Real wealth is the repertoire of physical transformations a person can cause — Deutsch's definition — so being able to repair a machine one would otherwise replace is a genuine increase in that repertoire. GDP, meanwhile, only counts transactions. The two move in opposite directions precisely because knowledge creation is not, in general, a transaction; it is a change in what someone can do. An economic measure that cannot register a repair as wealth is not measuring wealth at all, and this is the deep reason prosperity metrics systematically understate the effect of better explanations.

David Deutsch @DavidDeutschOxf
🗓️ Published on September 12, 2026

"@juanbenet Give up trying to measure knowledge or creativity. Correct the restraints and constraints and brakes and sabotage and punishments instead."

🔑 Key Takeaway

This redirects attention from the unmeasurable to the actionable. Knowledge and creativity cannot be quantified because they are not quantities — they are dispositions to cause transformations, and any attempt to score them ends up measuring a proxy that institutions then game. But restraints, constraints and punishments are enumerable and removable. The advice is therefore not a retreat into vagueness but a relocation of effort: stop trying to index the thing you want and start eliminating the known obstacles to it. That is the difference between measuring and creating, and it is the reason removing a brake reliably beats building a metric.

David Deutsch @DavidDeutschOxf
🗓️ Published on September 12, 2026

"@doncoffee26 Inverse broken window fallacy. I'm better off. The manufacturer is worse off. It's not a wash because I can spend that money on something else."

🔑 Key Takeaway

The broken window fallacy's inverse confirms the original principle. Standard broken-window thinking claims destruction stimulates the economy because the repairer spends; that is wrong because the money would have been spent on something new. Here the repair is genuine wealth creation — fixing my own thing — so I gain a transformation for money I would otherwise have spent on a replacement, freeing the difference for yet another purchase. The manufacturer loses that sale, so it is not a wash; but the point is that wealth is not conserved. New capabilities and freed spending are both created, which is exactly what a fallacy of fixed total wealth gets wrong.
