"Fallibilism: the third way. There is something to be objectively wrong about. Always."

🔑 Key Takeaway

Hall compresses the whole epistemology into a triad. Fallibilism is the ‘third way’ between two sterile dogmatisms — the sceptic who believes no view can ever be right, and the absolutist who believes some views are beyond correction. The radical move is the second clause: fallibilism is only meaningful if there is something to be objectively wrong about — always. That is, there exist real facts of the matter, independent of what anyone believes, such that your theory can simply be mistaken; and yet every claim, including your best one, is corrigible. This is not relativism and not certainty; it is the position that makes error-correction possible at all, because you need an objective wrongness to correct toward. It dissolves the popular confusion that saying ‘we could be wrong’ implies ‘nothing is right or wrong’ — precisely the opposite: the possibility of error presupposes an objective target. It is a good, hard-to-vary formulation of why critical rationalism is not a retreat into doubt but the exact engine of progress.

"@OttoGradkozy @stevenstrogatz The four-colour proof was later shortened, but still doesn't explain why four colours suffice. Finding such an explanation is still an open problem. Time was when a proof could be created only if someone understood it. That is no longer so."

🔑 Key Takeaway

Deutsch draws a surgical distinction between proving a proposition and explaining it. The four-colour theorem is proven — originally by an exhaustive computer check that no human could individually survey — but even after being shortened, the proof does not explain *why* four colours suffice; finding that explanation remains open. The final line is the epistemological shock: ‘Time was when a proof could be created only if someone understood it. That is no longer so.’ This marks a genuine rupture in the history of knowledge — the production of mathematical truth has been decoupled from human understanding of it. But, read through the error-correction lens, the point is precisely that explanation and proof are different things, and that a world where proofs outrun understanding raises the stakes on the very thing Deutsch values most: the hard-to-vary *explanation*, the understanding of why, which still cannot be automated the way mechanical verification of a lengthy case-check can.

David Deutsch @DavidDeutschOxf
🗓️ Published on November 24, 2026

"@bnielson01 @codybaldwin The immune system does create knowledge but not explanatory knowledge."

🔑 Key Takeaway

Deutsch refines the definition of *explanatory* knowledge by way of a telling example. The immune system does create knowledge — it produces antibodies that, in a sense, ‘know’ how to neutralise a pathogen, and it learns new defences through variation and selection. But this is not *explanatory* knowledge: the immune system does not understand why the pathogen is harmful, does not form theories, and cannot generalise its defence to predict a novel antigen it has never seen. This is the key that resolves the personhood debate: knowledge created by evolution, by search, by statistical pattern-matching all counts as knowledge in a broad sense, but only persons create explanatory knowledge — knowledge of *why*, expressible, aim-directed, and universalisable. The distinction is not a snobbery about ‘real’ knowledge; it is the precise boundary that separates a mindlessly improving process (whether an immune system or a large language model) from an explainer who understands. And it is that uniquely human capacity that no amount of reliable search reproduces.

The remark is a reminder that the quality of a decision-process depends on whether disagreement can be stated plainly, without a smile.

David Deutsch @DavidDeutschOxf
🗓️ Published on November 23, 2026

"@DanielJHannan From a contradiction you can deduce anything. And they do."

🔑 Key Takeaway

Deutsch deploys the classical logical principle of explosion against a regime that has built itself on contradictory premises. ‘From a contradiction you can deduce anything’ is the formal law — in classical logic, once a theory contains a single contradiction, the entire system collapses because any statement whatsoever can be derived from it, rendering the theory useless for distinguishing truth from falsehood. The dry second clause, ‘And they do,’ is the point: institutions that operate on mutually inconsistent beliefs do not merely hold an abstract flaw, they actively exploit it, spinning out whichever conclusion suits the moment. Read through the error-correction lens, this is the deepest argument for intellectual honesty — contradictory explanations are not a harmless mix of error and insight but a standing licence to justify anything. A society, an organisation, or a science that tolerates contradictions has disabled its error-elimination machinery, because no claim can ever be refuted once any conclusion is permitted. Consistency is not a luxury; it is the precondition for knowledge to progress.
