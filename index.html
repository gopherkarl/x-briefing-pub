Brett Hall (ToK Teacher) @ToKTeacher
🗓️ Published on September 21, 2026

"@prerat “Probably this will happen” is no prediction. It’s a prophecy. A prediction says what *will* happen given a good explanation. If I want to know when the killer asteroid hits (a prediction!), it’s of no use saying “probably once every 10,000 years given existing data”."

🔑 Key Takeaway

A crisp distinction between a prophecy and a prediction, where the difference is explanatory, not probabilistic. To say 'probably this will happen' is to attach a likelihood without a mechanism - a statistical guess that stays true no matter what the world actually does, and so explains nothing and rules nothing out. A genuine prediction, by contrast, is a deduction from a good explanation: it says what will happen under specified conditions, and it is the kind of thing that can be refuted. The force of the example is that a probability statement cannot tell you *when* - it cannot answer the question the way a physical theory can. The move separates knowledge (testable mechanism) from mere confidence (unfalsifiable odds).

"So “it’s just a stochastic parrot” is a bad critique - and refuting it doesn’t move the needle among those of us who say: “it’s not a person. It’s not becoming more like a person and it’s not about to become something even more creative that can out-think a person”."

🔑 Key Takeaway

Two distinct criticisms of AI are being separated rather than conflated. The 'stochastic parrot' charge is an epistemic one - it says the system only recombines patterns and so produces no genuine explanation. Refuting that charge (say, by showing the system can generate new explanatory content) does nothing to settle the deeper claim, which is that the system is not a person: it does not choose, originate, or have purposes in the Deutschian sense, and scaling does not push it closer to personhood. The post insists on keeping these refutations apart, because defeating the weaker objection is often mistaken for defeating the stronger one. What is being protected is the position that there is a categorical line - agents versus non-agents - that no amount of cleverness on the other side crosses.

David Deutsch @DavidDeutschOxf
🗓️ Published on September 21, 2026

"Could it be that most people have never understood a scientific explanation because they have never been told of a scientific problem and have been told that their own ones are their fault? (Same with maths? And philosophy? And political philosophy? British Constitution?)"

🔑 Key Takeaway

A diagnosis of why so few people feel they grasp science: they were never introduced to the *problem* an explanation solves, only to the finished formula, and were told their own confusions were their fault for not getting it. But an explanation is only intelligible against the problem it answers - present the mystery and the surprise, and the resolution becomes graspable; present the bare claim, and it stays a memorised assertion. The post generalises the point across disciplines - mathematics, philosophy, political philosophy, even the British constitution are opaque to the same degree that their motivating problems are hidden. The implied cure is to teach problems first. It reads as an argument about the conditions under which knowledge can actually be transmitted rather than merely recited.

"@SCHIZO_FREQ Hollywood figured this out. All of the money is in sequels - they cost more, they’re lower quality, but people want them anyway. True with movies, books, funds, even tweets."

🔑 Key Takeaway

The observation that the biggest returns come from repackaging the familiar rather than from novelty, and that markets reward it even though the output is lower quality. Sequels cost more and are usually worse, yet they reliably earn - because attention and capital follow what people already recognise, not what surprises them. Naval broadens the pattern from movies to books, funds, and even tweets: the thing that is easiest to sell is a credible iteration on a known identity or an established track record, not a genuinely new idea. The sting is that this describes business and signalling, not knowledge-creation - the most commercially efficient move is often the least epistemically valuable one, which is precisely why genuine discovery has to be pursued for its own sake rather than for the market.

The argument reframes the political contest as a contest over the *mechanism* of control, not over labels.
