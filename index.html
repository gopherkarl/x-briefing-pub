🔑 Key Takeaway

A discipline for separating the observation from the explanation of it. Hall compares the contested phenomenon to dark matter: it is real and observed, yet unexplained - and the temptation is to disguise that with extra labels ('parochial statement', 'point of view') as if naming it explained it. He refuses: the observation just is; we don't know why; and a label is the name of a problem, not a solution that explains itself. This is the popperian core - a good explanation is one that actually accounts for the phenomenon (hard to vary, connected to how the world works), whereas a dismissive label merely asserts a stance without accounting for the evidence. The post is a warning against mistaking rhetoric for knowledge and an insistence that an unexplained regularity stays open for real explanation rather than being closed off by a pejorative tag.

'Almost begs the question' flags that the benchmark is close to that failure, and the remedy is to adopt a standard that is not settled by one's own assumptions - a point with deep reach for how we evaluate AI capabilities.

David Deutsch @DavidDeutschOxf
🗓️ Published on September 25, 2026

"@NimeeshPatel @TBrit90 The immediate causes of WW2 were German militarism and dysfunctional political culture, and the Allies' appeasement policy. The Pattern in the West only contributed in several ways to weakening the Allies militarily."

🔑 Key Takeaway

A separation of immediate causes from contributory conditions. Deutsch distinguishes what actually caused the war - German militarism, a dysfunctional political culture, and appeasement - from the background factor ('the Pattern') whose role was limited to weakening the Allies militarily in several ways. This is the popperian move of getting the explanation right and hard to vary: a cause is that whose removal would have prevented the effect, whereas a contributory factor only altered severity or timing. It refuses the tempting overreach of crediting every disliked feature of the West with causal power it does not have. The post models precision in historical explanation - distinguishing the true movers from mere conditions - and quietly refutes both the denial of German responsibility and the exaggeration of Western institutional blame.

🔑 Key Takeaway

An observation about the misallocation of epistemic authority. The point is that society grants wide discretionary trust to politicians - who are, more often than not, competent at little - while refusing literal experts (engineers, technologists) the scope to work in their own field of expertise. The sting is in the final line: even the experts don't trust themselves, meaning the distorted hierarchy is internalised by those it wrongs. The principle underscored is the popperian one that authority should follow demonstrated knowledge and problem-solving capacity, not office or status. Where trust is granted to the least reliable and withheld from the most, an institution - whether a firm, a media, or a policy apparatus - systematically blocks the growth of knowledge by silencing its best conjectures.

""Ignorance, malice and wickedness". Though Mr Micron says it was a mistranslation. I expect it was. Perhaps he was only guilty of one or two of those vices not all three. https://t.co/dR7YHV5DDe"

🔑 Key Takeaway

Dry wit carrying a serious point about the difference between categories of error. Deutsch quotes three distinct failing modes - ignorance, malice, wickedness - and with mock-concession allows that the accused may have been guilty of only one or two, not all three. The serious content is that these are genuinely different and must not be conflated: ignorance is a lack of knowledge (remediable by better explanations), malice is deliberate harm (not remediable by more knowledge), and wickedness is a character failing of a graver kind. Attributing the wrong one changes both the moral diagnosis and the remedy - calling something wicked when it is really ignorant substitutes condemnation for correction. The joke models how precise distinctions, even in insult, sharpen understanding and keep criticism from collapsing into mere abuse.

Naval Ravikant @naval
🗓️ Published on September 24, 2026

"Every functioning institution is merit-based, not participation-based."

🔑 Key Takeaway

A crisp definitional claim about what makes an institution work. A functioning institution selects the people who best perform its function, whatever that function is - it weights contribution and capability, not membership or presence. A participation-based institution rewards showing up and belonging regardless of performance, which erodes the very competence that lets the institution do its job. The claim is testable because it names the selection criterion: look at who rises and who is retained, and you can tell which basis the institution actually runs on. It also generalises across institutions - companies, laboratories, armies, even epistemically, where the institution that 'functions' is the one whose ideas are selected by merit (correction) rather than by who asserts them.
