Brett Hall (ToK Teacher) @ToKTeacher
🗓️ Published on November 9, 2026

"@IAI_TV "Brain as computer" isn't a metaphor. The brain is *literally* a computer. It's not an analogy, simile, metaphor nor any other figure of speech. It's a law of physics. Disagree? Propose new physics - refute the Church-Turing-Deutsch principle."

🔑 Key Takeaway

Brett Hall makes the strongest possible version of the computationalist claim: calling the brain a computer is not a metaphor but a statement of physics. The load-bearing move is the Church-Turing-Deutsch principle — the assertion (from Deutsch's 'The Fabric of Reality') that any physical system whose behaviour can be simulated to arbitrary accuracy by a universal computer, and that obeys the laws of physics, can itself be regarded as performing computation. On this view, a physical process and its simulation by a universal machine are not merely analogous; they share the same underlying substrate of information-processing laws. Hall's challenge is deliberately falsifiable — 'propose new physics' — which is precisely the Popperian form of a good explanation: it stakes out a claim definite enough to be refuted, rather than a hedged metaphor that can shrug off any counter-example.

🔑 Key Takeaway

Thatcher diagnoses the European project not as an organic outgrowth but as a constructed, top-down design — a 'result of plans'. The contrast with a Deutschian critique of social engineering is precise: a polity ordered by a master plan rather than by error-correcting institutions treats society as a blueprint to be executed, not a set of problems to be solved adaptively. The phrase 'monument to the vanity of intellectuals' targets the presumption that a few minds can design the whole social order in advance. And 'the scale of the final damage done is in doubt' is the giveaway of a utopian programme: its failure is treated as certain in kind, unknowable only in magnitude — the signature refusal to learn and correct that distinguishes a closed, plan-based system from an open, problem-solving one.

🔑 Key Takeaway

Thatcher states the epistemological ground of prosperity: it is produced, never decreed. The Burke citation is the historical spine of the argument: the same 'fondness for trickery and short-cuts' that Burke diagnosed in revolutionaries who tried to *evade* difficulty is the attitude of those who promise prosperity without effort, whether through inflation, redistribution or central planning. The implied contrast with the Popperian frame is that genuine progress comes from tackling problems rather than evading them — that every short-cut that avoids an explanatory difficulty simply defers it, and the only reliable 'road' is sustained, error-correcting effort.

David Deutsch @DavidDeutschOxf
🗓️ Published on November 8, 2026

"@MohadBaboli "Bad regardless" is ambiguous. It could mean that there are no possible circumstances under which it is morally right to destroy the Iranian institute. Or it could mean that taken by itself without regard to circumstances, the event is regrettable. The latter may be true. The"

🔑 Key Takeaway

A model of how to dissolve a false dilemma by attending to ambiguity. Deutsch's interlocutor said a given act was 'bad regardless', and Deutsch insists the phrase carries two very different claims: that there are no possible circumstances under which the action could be morally right, or merely that, judged in isolation, the event is unfortunate. The first is a strong, universal moral prohibition; the second is a weak contextual judgement that leaves open whether circumstances could justify it. The analytical payoff is that most 'bad regardless' claims actually collapse to the weak reading — and once the ambiguity is exposed, the strong claim can no longer be smuggled in under the blanket of a vague phrase. It is the same principle as his 'entitled to judge / entitled to lie' correction: precise words are what make criticism testable, and testability is what makes criticism worth having.

David Deutsch @DavidDeutschOxf
🗓️ Published on November 7, 2026

"@jaybny @brockm It's hard to contradict innuendo. The factual allegations are exaggerated and stated tendentiously in a way that only makes sense on the *premise* that Israel is sinister, which is the purported conclusion. Beneath that premise is an assumption that saving Israeli lives is not"

🔑 Key Takeaway

A diagnosis of how a bad explanation protects itself from refutation. Deutsch points out that innuendo is hard to contradict because it evades the burden of a definite factual claim that could be tested — its 'factual allegations' are exaggerated and framed tendentiously. More sharply, he exposes the logical circle: the presentation only coheres if one already assumes the sinister conclusion ('Israel is sinister') that it purports to establish, since the same actions look innocent under a neutral premise. This is the textbook shape of an unfalsifiable explanation — an account that cannot be wrong because it is built on its own conclusion. For the feed it is also a moral-epistemology point: beneath the tendentious framing sits the assumption that not even saving Israeli lives is legitimate, a premise that is itself a conclusion about what an agent may do, smuggled in as if it were a premise.

Naval Ravikant @naval
🗓️ Published on November 6, 2026

"You can be either loved *and* hated, or merely liked and tolerated."

🔑 Key Takeaway

A compressed claim about the structure of taking a definite position. Naval notes that genuine conviction tends to polarise — you attract both strong love and strong hatred — whereas the cost of universal approval is being merely tolerated, i.e. never meaning enough to anyone to attract either. The underlying idea is that a clear, hard-to-vary stance (in the object of one's work or values) filters an audience into those who deeply value it and those who oppose it, while a safe, ambiguous stance buys anodyne acceptance at the price of significance. It fits the feed's epistemology because it reads the truth that a genuinely explanatory or principled position refuses to optimise for universal likability — which is itself a form of adherence to reality over audience, welcoming refutation from critics as much as support from allies.

"In our philosophy, the purpose of the life of the individual is not to be the servant of the State and its objectives, but to make the best of his talents and qualities. The sense of being self-reliant, of playing a role within the family, of owning one's own property, of paying"

🔑 Key Takeaway

An inversion of the default moral ordering of individual and state. Thatcher's premise is teleological — the 'purpose of the life of the individual' — and she insists that purpose is not to serve the State's objectives but to develop one's own talents and qualities. This is the classic agency-centred picture: the state is instrumental, justified only to the extent that it secures the conditions (self-reliance, family, property, voluntary civic contribution) under which individuals can exercise their agency, and it has no ends of its own that trump theirs. Casting the individual as the end and the state as the means is the opposite of collectivism's framing, and it grounds liberal institutions in a theory of what persons are for — an idea that this feed reads as continuous with the Popperian insistence that social institutions are problem-solving instruments whose value is judged by how well they serve free individuals, not by how well individuals serve them.

"Actual progress comes from hard-to-build technology. Which comes from competitive, creative, free-market capitalism. Which comes from one small, precarious area and then diffuses to the rest of the world. Snuff that out, and it’s global stagnation for an extremely long time."

🔑 Key Takeaway

A chain of causation running from difficult engineering to global progress. Naval's logic is that the kind of progress that actually transforms lives originates in technology that is *hard* to build — precisely because difficulty is a filter that only sustained, error-correcting productive effort can overcome — and that such effort is produced by a competitive, creative free-market capitalism. The chain is completed by the observation that this dynamism originates in 'one small, precarious area and then diffuses': innovation is not evenly distributed but born in a fragile margin whose freedom is its seedbed. The warning is the contrapositive — destroy that seedbed and the consequence is not gradual decline but stagnation 'for an extremely long time', because the conditions for regenerating the engine of progress themselves become scarce.
