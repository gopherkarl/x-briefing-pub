Brett Hall (ToK Teacher) @ToKTeacher
🗓️ Published on September 15, 2026

"@jbarro “AI Agents” deserve no rights. But a human mind *is software* running on the hardware that is the brain. That’s just the Church-Turing principle. Many don’t like it. But then many don’t like the second law of thermodynamics or the constancy of the speed of light, either."

🔑 Key Takeaway

Two questions are being prised apart here that are constantly conflated. The first is descriptive: what is a mind? If the Church-Turing principle holds, then any physically realisable computation can in principle be run on any sufficient hardware, and a human mind is software running on the brain rather than something categorically different from computation. The second question is normative: what has moral standing? Nothing about the first answer entails granting rights to “AI agents”, because rights belong to the kind of entity that creates and criticises explanations and can be held responsible for what it does — not to software that merely instantiates a process. The rhetorical move worth watching is the one that uses the truth of the first claim to smuggle in the second. That a theory describes something is never on its own a reason to treat it as a person; and, as the comparison with thermodynamics and the speed of light suggests, objections to an unwelcome explanation are not refutations of it.

"Stagnation, stasis, decline…that’s the real danger. Apocalypse talk is all fun and games until *that* happens. Good to hear that from *someone* on the mainstream! Nice work @benshapiro !"

🔑 Key Takeaway

The pessimistic imagination is fixated on the dramatic ending and blind to the mundane one. A civilisation does not need to be destroyed by catastrophe to fail; it can simply stop solving problems — accumulating unsolved difficulties, ossifying its institutions, and slowly losing the capacity for error-correction. Stagnation is the more probable failure mode precisely because it is undramatic: nobody is forced to notice it, and each individual step of retreat looks reasonable. This is why apocalypse talk is not merely exaggerated but misdirected, since the disaster it points at would at least be visible, whereas stasis arrives without an event to date it from. The optimistic corollary is that the remedy is not better prophecy but the reopening of problem-solving.

🔑 Key Takeaway

The first thing to ask of any proposal to regulate a technology is whose interests the rule would actually protect. Where incumbents face a smaller compliance burden than entrants, a rule ostensibly written for safety functions as a barrier to competition — the cost of entry becomes the moat. The second, distinct criticism is methodological: a claim presented as a series of assertions with no evidence and no argument is not a conjecture that can be criticised, tested, or refuted, and therefore not knowledge. Note that these are independent objections — an argument can be sincerely motivated by safety and still be unfalsifiable, and a well-argued claim can still be captured by incumbents — which is why both must be examined separately.

"The Labour Party’s proposals on devolution threaten chaos, and possibly the dissolution of the Union of the United Kingdom itself. Moreover, by embracing European federalism — through the European social chapter and, above all, the European single currency — a Labour government could deal a terminal blow to the traditions of British parliamentary democracy. Traditionally, the Socialists believed that the State must make people equal; though an honest look at the perks and privileges of the Communist nomenklatura might have set them right about that. The New-look Labour Party now apparently wants the State to make people highminded and socially aware; though a thought for how difficult the Churches find it to change people’s behaviour ought to induce some doubts when mere politicians start to preach. So the limitation of government is still the great issue of British politics — and indeed to a remarkable degree of global politics. The threat to limited government did not end with the collapse of communism and the discrediting of socialism. It remains an issue in Western — particularly European — democracies. There is a constant tendency, in which pressure groups, vested interests and the media play a part, for government to expand. One of Thatcher’s laws — for which I owe something to Lord Acton — is that all government tends to expand, and Socialist government expands absolutely. If you start with their view of the State — that it exists to right social wrongs rather than to create a framework for freedom — you can never find the definitive justification for saying 'no'. Above all, you cannot say 'no' to demands for more spending on welfare. That is why in Sweden the share of national income the government took reached some seventy per cent. It’s why it’s several points higher in Europe on average than here."

🔑 Key Takeaway

The argument identifies a structural asymmetry rather than a moral failing of any particular party. Once the state is conceived as the instrument for righting social wrongs, there is no principled stopping point: every new wrong generates a fresh claim on state power, and no claim can be refused on the state's own terms. A constitution built on creating a framework for freedom, by contrast, supplies criteria for saying ‘no’. Hence Lord Acton's tendency generalised into a law — all government tends to expand, and governments that begin from the correction of social wrongs expand without limit, which is what makes welfare spending the point at which restraint is hardest to argue for. The warning about devolution and European federalism is best read in the same terms: a reallocation of powers whose consequences for parliamentary accountability, not just for the party balance, are the thing to assess.

David Deutsch @DavidDeutschOxf
🗓️ Published on September 14, 2026

"@JonathanWaxman1 Popper's criterion is not whether the "public [picks] out the best, you know, ideas and people each time". It is how easy it is for RULERS (particularly GOVERNMENTS, not people, not candidates, not legislators) to be removed without violence or the threat thereof."

🔑 Key Takeaway

A correction of a very common misreading. Popper's criterion for a good political system is not that the electorate chooses well — it is a structural property of the institutions: whether those who hold power can be removed without violence. Framing it as a test of voter wisdom makes the criterion unusable, because no electorate is wise (or unwise) often enough to decide the matter, and it invites evaluating democracy by outcomes it was never designed to guarantee. What the criterion actually identifies is the mechanism that makes error-correction possible in politics at all: if rulers cannot be got rid of peacefully, then criticism carries no consequences and mistakes can only accumulate. Democracy is thereby justified as an instrument for removing bad rulers, not as a means of finding good ones.

David Deutsch @DavidDeutschOxf
🗓️ Published on September 14, 2026

"The word 'war' has become a mere intensifier. But in reality a war crime is something that in many cases wouldn't be a crime unless there had been special treaties making it one. A prisoner of war is someone who can legitimately be kept in prison until hostilities are over. https://t.co/fxsXFNo2ds"

🔑 Key Takeaway

A reminder that many of the categories we treat as natural kinds are artefacts of human invention, sustained only by explicit agreement. “War crime” and “prisoner of war” have no content except what treaties give them: absent the conventions, what the words denote would be an ordinary crime or an arbitrary detention. This is not a debunking of international law but an explanation of what it is — a constructed framework that has to be maintained by institutions and norms, and which dissolves if they are abandoned. The inflation of ‘war’ into a rhetorical intensifier (“war on X”) degrades exactly the precision on which those frameworks depend, which is why the language we use about conflict is not merely decorative.

Naval Ravikant @naval
🗓️ Published on September 14, 2026

""When liability exposure approaches 0—when no one pays the price for unverified failures—verification budgets collapse...Deployers flood the Runaway Risk Zone with unmonitored agents, capturing the gains of automation while socializing catastrophic risks." The original paper is linked in the following post: https://x.com/naval/status/2099476024979317096"

🔑 Key Takeaway

The argument is a mechanism, not a moral complaint, and that is what makes it useful: liability is the price signal that causes anyone to spend money on verification. Set the exposure to zero and the incentive to discover failure modes — i.e. the incentive to create the knowledge of what can go wrong — goes to zero too, while the gains of deployment remain private and the costs become public. This is the same asymmetry that makes safety regulation hard in every other domain, and it explains why voluntarism fails without requiring anyone to assume that the actors are reckless: the structure rewards not finding out. An optimum is not maximal liability either; the point is that some binding cost must exist or the error-correction vanishes.
