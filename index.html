"Imagine thinking super abundance and wealth resulting from AI (and say the creation of fusion power) would cause CEOs to say “let's fire almost everyone” rather than “let's award most bonuses while expanding and hiring way more to do more of this great stuff”."

🔑 Key Takeaway

A refutation of the scarcity-driven fear that AI abundance will cause mass layoffs. The argument turns on what abundance actually incentivises: a CEO facing vastly more wealth and capability does not respond by making fewer, poorer products — they respond by expanding to do more of the great thing, hiring more and rewarding the people who made it happen. Firing almost everyone is the response to a shrinking, not an exploding, opportunity; it makes sense under scarcity and zero-sum thinking, not under super abundance. The frame is that fear of AI-induced unemployment imports a static, finite-pie model into a world that AI and fusion are making dynamic and expanding, and the error is to project today's constrained psychology onto a future of radically lower costs of creation. The testable claim: watch what creators of compounding abundance actually do with profits — hoard them against a contracting market, or reinvest them into more creation.

🔑 Key Takeaway

A recommendation of David Deutsch's account of coercion as a deeper treatment than what appears in contractarian and analytic political philosophy. The point being surfaced is that coercion is usually treated as a single blunt category — 'forcing someone to do something against their will' — whereas Deutsch unpacks it as a specific kind of conflict, one defined by the use of threats to make another choose an option they would otherwise reject. Understanding coercion this precisely matters because it determines what is and is not legitimate: if coercion is identifiable as a distinct mode of interaction rather than a vague synonym for 'influence' or 'power', then arguments about the state's monopoly on it become sharper. ToKTeacher flags it as worth reading because most philosophies using the term never actually define it well enough to do philosophical work with it.

"@pappapez Why are you sneering at the West, calling its history "propaganda" and yourself a "subject" of it?"

🔑 Key Takeaway

A challenge that refuses the dismissal of the West's history as propaganda. The question targets the reflexive disclaimer — 'it's all just their propaganda, I'm only a subject of it' — which lets the speaker avoid engaging with the actual content of Western history and ideals while positioning themselves as a detached victim rather than an agent. Deutsch's reply demands the sneerer take a stance: either the history is true (in which case 'propaganda' is a smear used to avoid learning from it) or it is false (in which case say what is false and why). The deeper point is that calling inconvenient truth 'propaganda' is a way of immunising oneself from having to correct one's own judgement — exactly the opposite of the critical attitude. Treating oneself as a passive 'subject' of history is the refusal of the agency that civilisation, and knowledge itself, depends on.
