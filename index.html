Open questions count as criticisms. Of course they don\u2019t tell you how to answer them: hence they\u2019re always \u201cpending\u201d."

🔑 Key Takeaway

A neat account of why 'pending criticism' is the normal and healthy condition of every explanation, not a defect. Every theory carries open questions - unresolved points that point toward its successors - and these open questions count as criticisms. The key refinement is that a criticism need not tell you how to answer it: it is 'always pending' precisely because registering an open question does not supply the solution, it merely marks where inquiry must go next. This dissolves the false standard that a good explanation must have no loose ends; rather, the ability of an idea to generate open questions is what makes progress possible, since each successor theory answers the previous one's open questions while opening new ones. It is a precise statement of how knowledge grows by error-elimination rather than by reaching a state with no further problems.

The deeper claim is that names encode theories: calling a system 'artificial intelligence' smuggles in assumptions about agency, mind, and understanding, whereas the older word honestly labeled the thing as a tool for labour. Choosing terminology is therefore choosing an implicit explanation, and the remark is a reminder to be alert to the load that labels carry - rename a tool 'intelligence' and you begin to argue about it as though it were a mind.

🔑 Key Takeaway

A criticism of the BBC for reviving a historical excuse that had already been tested and rejected by an authoritative review. The Bergier Commission - the official Swiss inquiry into the country's wartime conduct - examined precisely the claim that Switzerland returned refugees and barred others to keep Nazi Germany from invading and concluded it inadequately explained Switzerland's actions. Deutsch's point is that this is not an open question awaiting fresh inquiry but a settled episode of error-correction that the BBC is re-litigating: repeating an explanation that has already been shown false, without addressing the evidence that refuted it, is a failure of the critical attitude. It models how knowledge is meant to accumulate - once a conjecture is falsified by evidence, citing it again as though unchallenged is intellectual regression, and institutions that recycle such claims forfeit epistemic trust.

David Deutsch @DavidDeutschOxf
🗓️ Published on September 27, 2026

"A game that began with a trick: an opening that looks bad, and is bad, but nowhere near as bad as it looks. So unless the opponent knows *exactly* how to exploit it\u2026 https://t.co/VhX2LzEiF2"

🔑 Key Takeaway

An illustration, via a chess opening, of a deeper principle about knowledge and asymmetric advantage. The opening is a trick: it looks bad and genuinely is bad, but far less bad than it appears, so the entire value of the move depends on the opponent not knowing exactly how to exploit it. The mechanism is information asymmetry - assessment of a position is a matter of knowledge, and the player who understands the true degree of the error can profit against one who overestimates it. It is a small model of why understanding matters: identical board states can be winning or losing depending on what the player knows, because action is governed by explanation, not by appearances. In any situation that 'looks bad', how bad it really is is a question for knowledge, and the difference between guessing and knowing is precisely what separates the tricked from the tricker.

David Deutsch @DavidDeutschOxf
🗓️ Published on September 27, 2026

"What should I conclude if, after a few years, all famous mathematical problems are solved and the trickle of new ones by humans are solved immediately by AIs? That there is a severe problem with the best existing epistemology (Popper's) and that AIs should be tasked to solve it."

🔑 Key Takeaway

Deutsch offers a genuinely falsifying condition for his own philosophy. The prior debate was about what to conclude if the flow of AI-solved problems stops while human proofs continue; here he flips it: if instead AIs come to solve every famous and newly posed problem faster than humans can even pose them, that would be evidence of a severe defect in the best existing epistemology - that is, in Popper's account of how knowledge grows. The move is exemplary of the critical attitude he advocates: he does not defend his epistemology by making it immune to counterexample; he states precisely the observation that would force him to revise it. And the remedy is telling - task the AIs to solve the epistemological problem itself, because progress consists precisely in treating an anomaly as a problem to be dissolved rather than a fact to be accommodated.

"@BradIngarfield It's become vanishingly rare to hear someone talk about AI without the hype and remain \u201cgrounded\u201d (for want of another term) in epistemology and physics - rather than having pure flights of fantasy (which typically lead to the end of the world, of course)."

🔑 Key Takeaway

A diagnosis of why most AI commentary is worthless: it is ungrounded. Reasonable commentary on AI stays accountable to two fixed backstops - epistemology (what would count as knowledge or understanding, and whether the claimed capability is genuinely novel or merely pattern-completion) and physics (what the laws of nature actually permit). Hype cuts itself loose from both, which is why it so reliably concludes in apocalyptic fantasy: an imagination unconstrained by what can be known or what is physically possible has nowhere to land but in doom. The remark is itself a small method: to evaluate any claim about AI, ask whether it could in principle be checked, and whether it respects the constraints of reality - if it cannot and does not, it is poetry about the future, not a prediction. Grounding in physics and epistemology is what separates a hypothesis from a fable.
