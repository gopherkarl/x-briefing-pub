An argument that no explanation is inherently beyond human comprehension because explanations are made by and for persons. 'Explaining better is always a soluble problem' is the optimistic corollary: the difficulty of understanding is itself a problem, and problems are solvable by the creation of new knowledge.

"Assuming some explanation will be inherently incomprehensible to us ignores how explanations consist of connected statements. One can always ask “Where's the stumbling block? Step 1? Step n?” Ok, so get a deeper, better explanation of that step and proceed."

🔑 Key Takeaway

A practical method for dissolving the fear of incomprehensibility. Because an explanation is not a monolithic block but a chain of connected statements, any difficulty can be localised: ask where the stumbling block is — step 1 or step n — and then get a deeper, better explanation of just that step and proceed. This is error-correction applied to understanding itself: comprehension is not an all-or-nothing gift but a sequence of steps, each of which can be explained more clearly. The claim refutes the idea that some knowledge is sealed off from us, because any gap in understanding is a specific, addressable step rather than an impenetrable wall.

A diagnosis of utopianism as a recurring political error. A call to treat the failure to speak against evil as itself a phenomenon in need of explanation. Naming the gap in understanding is the first step toward closing it, because a force that is not understood cannot be resisted with knowledge.

"@MachinaHill Well, taken literally, they define away the whole physical and moral universe respectively. How much further-reaching would you like? Taken philosophically, they deny realism. That's pretty far-reaching as well: no Popper, no Einstein, no Everett, no Gödel even…"

🔑 Key Takeaway

A warning that certain philosophical positions, taken seriously, annihilate the very foundations of knowledge. Deutsch points out that two views — taken literally — 'define away the whole physical and moral universe respectively', and taken philosophically they deny realism, which would erase Popper, Einstein, Everett, and even Gödel. The argument is that realism is not an optional ornament but the precondition of all the great explanatory achievements: if the world is not really there and knowable, then no theory can be true and no error can be corrected. The remark is a defence of the deepest layer of the epistemic stack — the assumption that reality exists and is comprehensible, without which nothing else follows.

David Deutsch @DavidDeutschOxf
🗓️ Published on October 19, 2026

"Hmm. Problems are soluble, though. https://t.co/pKbzKjV8BR"

🔑 Key Takeaway

The critical-optimist creed compressed to its essence. Against any claim that some problem is insoluble, Deutsch's reply is that problems are soluble — not because every problem has already been solved, but because there is no known law of nature that forbids solving them, and the history of knowledge is the history of 'impossible' problems being solved. The word 'though' marks the refusal to accept defeatism as a description of reality rather than a failure of imagination. It is the principle that underlies all progress: the obstacle is always ignorance, and ignorance is always, in principle, removable by the creation of new explanations.
