David Deutsch @DavidDeutschOxf
🗓️ Published on November 25, 2026

"@nuberodesign Mathematical abstractions and techniques are used in virtually all human endeavours. That does not mean that physicists and engineers, farmers and ornithologists, are in any sense mathematicians. The latter try to understand those abstractions, and many others, themselves."

🔑 Key Takeaway

Deutsch draws a firm boundary around the identity 'mathematician'. Mathematical abstractions and techniques are used almost everywhere — by physicists, engineers, farmers, ornithologists — but using mathematics is not the same as being a mathematician. The distinguishing mark is not applicability but *aim*: the mathematician is the one who tries to understand those abstractions themselves, as objects of enquiry, rather than merely deploying them as tools. This is a hard-to-vary criterion because it separates a practice from the technical apparatus it happens to use. It matters for the AI-in-mathematics debate running through this feed: a program that compiles and applies known methods is deeply mathematical in the instrumental sense, yet it is not doing the mathematician's distinctive work unless it seeks to understand the abstractions themselves. Being able to run the technique is not achieving understanding of it.

Under the error-correction lens this is precisely a failure to engage the best available refutations: the dispute cannot advance if one side's objections ignore the other side's published conjectures and tests. It is also a reminder that testability is not a fixed property of a theory but something that changes as knowledge grows — a conjecture deemed untestable in one era becomes empirically addressable once someone thinks up an experiment that would refute it. The epistemic failure Hall is exposing is not the existence of disagreement but the refusal to update the criticism in the light of new knowledge.

🔑 Key Takeaway

Hall states the critical-rationalist attitude toward authority in its sharpest form: authority is a substitute for missing argument, not a foundation for a good one. If a claim had the facts on its side, it would not need to lean on the speaker's status as a scientist — the argument would stand on its own. The wry second clause, 'But it doesn't, so she does,' is the diagnostic: reliance on authority is itself evidence that the reasoning being defended is weak. This is not anti-science; it is anti-authoritarianism in the Popperian sense, where knowledge progresses by the quality of explanations and their exposure to criticism, never by who asserts them. The post is a compact test anyone can apply: the more the speaker signals credential, the more suspicious we should be that the case lacks independent warrant.

Hall offers a working definition of universality — the capacity to generate explanations of any physical process, any experience, anything that happens anywhere and anytime, in principle, in physical reality. It is deliberately not an exhaustive formal definition but an orienting characterisation of the scope of explanation: no physical phenomenon is in principle beyond our reach of understanding. This is the core of Deutsch's optimist epistemology and of the claim that humans are universal explainers. The interesting move is that Hall ties the concept to the possibility of error-correction: because explanations can be aimed at any physical process, nothing is permanently exempt from being understood, tested, and improved. The caution that it is 'not definitive' is itself a Popperian gesture — he offers it as a conjecture open to refinement rather than a closed dogma.

David Deutsch @DavidDeutschOxf
🗓️ Published on November 24, 2026

"@elonmusk Consciousness is a property that running software can have, not hardware."

🔑 Key Takeaway

Deutsch compresses an entire position on the mind into one sentence aimed at Musk: consciousness is a property of running software, not of hardware. The distinction matters because it dissolves a persistent category error — attributing mentality to the physical substrate (silicon, neurons) rather than to the causal structure of what is computed. Under this view, a brain matters not because it is made of biological tissue but because of the organised information-processing it implements; in principle, the same structure running on a different substrate could host the same mentality. This is why the personhood debate in this feed turns on explanatory knowledge and creative agency, not on whether some future computer is made of the right material. The hardware/software split also implies that consciousness is not automatically conferred by scale or brute computation; it depends on what the running program actually does, which is the lever for both understanding minds and evaluating claims about artificial sentience.

David Deutsch @DavidDeutschOxf
🗓️ Published on November 24, 2026

"@DStrungk It was important: it made all existing theories of light problematic. Showing someone the experiment without explanation would not have made them realise this. Einstein's theory explained it. (BTW he is thought not to have known of the experiment, though he later claimed he had!)"

🔑 Key Takeaway

Deutsch makes a precise point about what made the photoelectric experiment important: it made all existing theories of light problematic. The raw experimental result alone would not have imparted this — showing someone the experiment without explanation would not make them realise its significance. Only Einstein's theory explained it, converting a puzzling result into evidence that refuted all prior theories and supported a new explanation. This is a compact illustration that the same observation means different things under different theories, and that 'significance' is not a property of data but of the explanation that interprets it. It is also a small, quiet joke at the expense of hindsight: Einstein is thought not to have known of the experiment, though he later claimed he had — a reminder that even great explainers can misremember their own epistemic history, and that reconstruction after the fact is itself fallible.

Khemani highlights a razor-sharp strategic observation about the AI industry: NVIDIA must become a frontier lab faster than the frontier labs are becoming chip companies. This is an error-correcting, evolutionary framing of industrial competition — each side is racing to absorb the other's capabilities before the counterparty absorbs theirs. The chip giant cannot rest on selling compute; the model labs are becoming hardware designers, so the moat must be built both ways. Under the creativity-and-knowledge lens, it is an arms race in which the durable advantage is not the existing asset (a chip roadmap, a model) but the rate of learning and the ability to expand into adjacent knowledge rapidly. The winning firm is the one that outruns the obsolescence of its own comparative advantage by generating new explanations, which is the very definition of progress as escape from a predicted rut.
