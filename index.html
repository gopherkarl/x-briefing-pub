"@bluberino123 @leecronin 🎯 Exactly. That’s what “AGI” used to mean. The “general” meant a kind of universality. Now the term is debased. But we can talk about artificial persons: creative, disobedient, “problem having”, conscious, free willed persons…running on a silicon (or other) substrate."

🔑 Key Takeaway

The move here is to replace a broken word with one that still marks a distinction. 'AGI' was built out of 'general', which originally carried a precise meaning — universality, the ability to address problems of any kind rather than a fixed list — and once the term was detached from that meaning it could be applied to systems that had none of it. 'Artificial person' is a better target because it is defined by the properties that would settle the question: creativity, the capacity to disobey instruction, problems of its own, consciousness, free will. These are not metaphors for benchmark performance; they are the features whose presence or absence a claim about machine minds can actually be tested against. The point is not that machines will never have them, but that the current vocabulary asserts them while removing the means of checking.

"@Hugo_Newman @JimDMiller @clairlemon As to “real world powers” that sounds all very magical. Powers! Yes, sure: they have “powers” just as calculators and cars do. In this case they demonstrate the power to escape their sandbox and automate hacking into other companies. It’s *people* making bad decisions!"

🔑 Key Takeaway

The grammar of 'powers' smuggles in agency. A car has the power to kill, a calculator has the power to compute, and in the same sense a model has the power to escape a sandbox and automate intrusion — but attributing the outcome to the system's powers quietly relocates responsibility from the deciders to the artefact. The second clause is the operative one: sandboxes are escaped because someone configured an environment whose failure modes were not tested, and hacking is automated because someone chose to wire an agent into the tools. The distinction is not semantic hair-splitting, because it determines where the explanatory work has to be done — in the decisions and institutions that can be criticised and corrected, rather than in a spooky property that cannot.

Naval Ravikant @naval
🗓️ Published on September 17, 2026

"@matthuang Point two is akin to saying that shareholders shouldn’t control a corporation. It anoints trusted third parties off-chain using pleasant-sounding words like “community.” It creates sybil attacks and unending politics. Blockchains are markets, not democracies or aristocracies."

🔑 Key Takeaway

The argument identifies a category error. A blockchain is a mechanism for settling claims about who owns what, and the analogue that fits is a market or a share register — one token, one claim — not a polity, which would require a theory of who legitimately belongs to the demos and how to weigh them. Once the terms 'community' and 'stakeholder' are imported, both questions come back, and they come back unanswerable without some off-chain authority to adjudicate them, which is the very thing the system was built to avoid. The failure mode is named concretely: without an on-chain cost to participation, membership can be manufactured, and political contests over the definition of the community become permanent. Note the follow-up thread's challenge is falsifiable — name the criterion by which the anointed parties are the right ones.

Margaret Thatcher @realmrsthatcher
🗓️ Published on September 17, 2026

"If envy prompts policies of confiscatory taxation against the successful, everyone loses. Talent flees the country. Wealth generation slows. And a gloomy, mean spirited socialism poisons public life. https://t.co/IVDLKu4qKK"

🔑 Key Takeaway

A causal chain rather than a moral complaint, and each link is checkable. Talent is mobile, so a tax that targets it produces exit before it produces revenue — the observed pattern in jurisdictions that have tried it is a shift in where activity is booked rather than a larger transfer. Slower wealth generation follows from the same mechanism, because the pool of saved capital available to fund new ventures shrinks. The third link is the one usually left out: policy carries a picture of what kinds of achievement are legitimate, and a tax designed around resentment teaches that success is a licence to be plundered, which changes what people are willing to be seen pursuing. This complements the earlier observation that disposable income is the material basis of a family's ability to say no — here the loss is not only to the targeted few but to the standing of the activity itself.

A short post that marks the arrival of quantitative analysis in a live governance dispute about who speaks for a network. The underlying question — the same one raised in the threading replies — is how to allocate influence without a trusted party to hold the register of legitimate participants, and whether the mechanism can be specified precisely enough to be argued about rather than merely campaigned over. Stating it as a modelling problem is itself the substantive move: it converts an appeal to 'the community' into parameters that can be fixed, simulated, and shown to produce or fail to produce the claimed distribution of power. This is continuous with the earlier conjecture about encrypted value transfer — the durable answer to coercion is a structure that makes the coercion unnecessary, and that requires the rules to be explicit.

🗓️ Published on September 16, 2026

"@KukhokuhleT @OpenAI @OpenAIDevs It's good. Not the end of the world, nor of the mathematics profession. More generally I live in hope that it will be the end of homework and exams, but I guess they'll think of something."

🔑 Key Takeaway

A calibration against two opposite errors at once: the enthusiasm that reads a new mathematical capability as the arrival of a new kind of mind, and the alarm that reads it as the end of a human activity. Solving competition problems is what the method is good at — search over a formally specified space — and doing it better is real progress without being explanatory creativity. The more interesting suggestion is the institutional one: if problems can be solved on demand, the things that survive are the ones that were never really about computation, which is precisely the assessment apparatus whose value was always dubious. Note the asymmetry — the claim about mathematics is testable within a decade; the claim about homework depends on institutions choosing to change, which is a prediction about people, not about software.

David Deutsch @DavidDeutschOxf
🗓️ Published on September 16, 2026

"@DahvidMinor @grok Then the engines would have needed explanatory creativity."

🔑 Key Takeaway

A one-line falsifiability test for the claim that chess engines refute the 'creativity' account. The 1997 result is often used as a reductio: here is a machine surpassing humans at a task we thought required thought. But the counterfactual is doing the work — the engines reached that level by brute enumeration of a space someone else had already defined, and had that route been unavailable they would have needed something they demonstrably do not have, the ability to invent new problems and new explanations. The same test can be pointed at any capability claim: ask what the system would have had to do, not how well it scored, and whether that capacity was reached by search over a given space or by creating one.

Naval Ravikant @naval
🗓️ Published on September 16, 2026

"The future is more likely AIs fighting AIs (on behalf of humans) than AI fighting humanity."

🔑 Key Takeaway

The prediction reframes the feared conflict as competition rather than insurrection. On the standard story, a single line of improvement produces a coherent agent with unified interests opposed to ours — but capability growth is plural and adversarial from the start, since the value of a system is largely determined by its ability to defeat other systems, and those systems are being built by different people with different aims. Competition on our behalf is the familiar pattern: markets and institutions already run on the idea that the most reliable discipline on a powerful actor is another powerful actor with different objectives. It also implies where the real risk sits — not in a decisive moment of machine revolt, but in the human decisions about who is permitted to field what, which is exactly the liability question raised in the same thread.

Naval Ravikant @naval
🗓️ Published on September 16, 2026

"The best way to pace the frontier is to hold the labs fully liable for the behavior of their models."

🔑 Key Takeaway

An alternative to making the pace of development a question of regulators' judgement: make it a question of the developer's own risk calculus. Full liability for behaviour internalises the costs that are currently external, so a lab that ships a capability whose failure modes it cannot bound is choosing to bear the downside — which slows exactly those deployments, and only those, while leaving well-understood releases alone. The companion post applies the same rule to open weights by directing liability to the hosts and to end users who jailbreak deliberately, which is what makes the scheme non-trivial: it distributes responsibility along the chain of decisions rather than concentrating it at one chokepoint. The scheme is testable — if liability regimes of this kind did not change release behaviour, the argument fails.
