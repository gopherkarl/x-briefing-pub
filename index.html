"More than a 99.999% chance we enter the greatest era in human history: solve aging, harness infinite energy, build flying cities, conquer the stars, uncover the deepest secrets of the universe. Yes, I made that number up. But we can genuinely make it happen."

🔑 Key Takeaway

The number is admittedly invented, and the author says so — which is the interesting part. The claim that carries weight is not the probability but the possibility: everything listed is permitted by the laws of physics, and nothing but a lack of knowledge stands between us and it. That is creative optimism in its proper form, a conjecture about what is achievable, not a prophecy about what will happen. Progress is not forecast; it is made by people who take the possibility seriously enough to work on it.

🔑 Key Takeaway

The distinction that matters is between knowing and believing, and it turns on whether an explanation has survived criticism rather than on how strongly it is held. Since the future is uncreated, no one can know what will happen; what we can have is a good explanation of what is possible and why. 'Belief' enters precisely where an objectively good explanation is missing, and it is a poor substitute: it supplies conviction without the error-correction that makes knowledge worth having.

🔑 Key Takeaway

Force is justified here instrumentally, by the peace it preserves rather than by the conflict it wins. The mechanism is a costly signal: what deters is the credible expectation that aggression will fail, so weakness is not merely risky but actively inviting. This is a narrow, testable claim about incentives rather than a general glorification of strength — and its failure mode is the opposite error, arms held for their own sake.

🔑 Key Takeaway

The defence of precise language is therefore part of the defence of criticism itself.
