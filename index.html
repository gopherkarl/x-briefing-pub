"What is inevitable are problems. But problems are soluble. See the work of @DavidDeutschOxf - in particular "The Beginning of Infinity". Again *we* personally or as a civilisation can be such a beginning. But nothing is guaranteed as all extinct civilisations demonstrate."

🔑 Key Takeaway

The compressed Popperian-Deutschian thesis in one post. Problems are a permanent feature of existence - no solution removes the possibility of new problems, so they are indeed inevitable. But every problem is in principle solvable by new knowledge; there is no problem that is insoluble in principle, only problems whose solutions we have not yet created. The reference to The Beginning of Infinity and the warning that nothing is guaranteed (all extinct civilisations demonstrate) keep the optimism critical rather than naive: it is not a promise that progress is automatic but an invitation to be the kind of agent that solves problems. The testable claim is that the failure mode is not 'problems exist' but 'we stopped trying to solve them' - and that a civilisation can itself be a beginning of infinity or, like the extinct ones, a failure to be one.

"Progress, and accelerating progress at that, is possible. But it's not inevitable. We can fail. So we have to put effort into solving our problems. The laws of physics do not prohibit us from becoming immortal and reaching the stars. But they do not guarantee it either. https://t.co/DxbjrH0prH"

🔑 Key Takeaway

A clean separation of possibility from inevitability. The laws of physics do not prohibit immortality or reaching the stars, so those outcomes are possible; but they do not guarantee them either, so they are not inevitable. Progress therefore requires effort - the creation of knowledge - and can fail. This is the critical-optimist stance: optimism is not the prediction that things will get better on their own but the claim that they can, combined with the insistence that making them better is our responsibility. The reference to failure and to the possibility of not reaching the stars is the refutation of inevitability: if progress were automatic, no civilisation would ever have collapsed, and no goal would ever be missed. The post refuses both the doomer's 'it is fated to fail' and the accelerationist's 'it is fated to succeed', leaving the outcome to the quality of our explanations and the effort we invest.

"@AlexanderKalian To go from "We are designing AI that will follow our instructions ever better" to "It will soon diverge from us and disobey" is also quite the leap."

🔑 Key Takeaway

A refutation of the doomer leap by exposing a missing mechanism. The premise of current AI development is that we are designing systems that follow instructions ever better - that is, increasing compliance. The doomer conclusion is that it will soon diverge from us and disobey. The leap is that there is no mechanism connecting better instruction-following to eventual disobedience; the two claims are about different capacities, and the inference from one to the other is a non-sequitur. If the very thing being optimised is obedience, then the claim that the system will spontaneously become disobedient needs an account of how that transition happens - and none is supplied. The post is a demand that the doomer narrative provide a mechanism rather than an unfalsifiable prophecy, which is the same standard applied to any genuine prediction.

An admission of a failed prediction that itself models the error-correcting attitude. David Deutsch @DavidDeutschOxf
🗓️ Published on September 21, 2026

"Could it be that most people have never understood a scientific explanation because they have never been told of a scientific problem and have been told that their own ones are their fault? (Same with maths? And philosophy? And political philosophy? British Constitution?)"

🔑 Key Takeaway

A diagnosis of why so few people feel they grasp science: they were never introduced to the *problem* an explanation solves, only to the finished formula, and were told their own confusions were their fault for not getting it. But an explanation is only intelligible against the problem it answers - present the mystery and the surprise, and the resolution becomes graspable; present the bare claim, and it stays a memorised assertion. The post generalises the point across disciplines - mathematics, philosophy, political philosophy, even the British constitution are opaque to the same degree that their motivating problems are hidden. The implied cure is to teach problems first. It reads as an argument about the conditions under which knowledge can actually be transmitted rather than merely recited.

🔑 Key Takeaway

The observation that the biggest returns come from repackaging the familiar rather than from novelty, and that markets reward it even though the output is lower quality. Sequels cost more and are usually worse, yet they reliably earn - because attention and capital follow what people already recognise, not what surprises them. Naval broadens the pattern from movies to books, funds, and even tweets: the thing that is easiest to sell is a credible iteration on a known identity or an established track record, not a genuinely new idea. The sting is that this describes business and signalling, not knowledge-creation - the most commercially efficient move is often the least epistemically valuable one, which is precisely why genuine discovery has to be pursued for its own sake rather than for the market.

🔑 Key Takeaway

Distinguishes the ordinary harm of a fad from a distinctly moral harm. Any enthusiasm can mildly strain relations between the eager and the indifferent. Naming this as 'the Pattern' connects it to a recognisable diagnosis that recurs across ideologies - the recruitment of personal loyalty into institutionalised enmity. On this framing, what matters is not the particular slogans but the standing invitation to see those closest as enemies, which is a testable claim about consequences rather than a matter of taste.
