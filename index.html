An argument that the true cause of inflation is monetary, not administered. It reframes inflation as a consequence of fiscal indiscipline rather than a technical accident of the price level.

An exposure of the reasoning error behind trusting 'the paper of record' on unfamiliar topics. ToKTeacher's reply to @elidourado is that calling a specific outlet 'the paper of record' casts it as a reliable source on subjects one knows little about — precisely where the risk of error is highest. 'Gell-Mann amnesia' is the name for the phenomenon in which a reader can see an error in an article on a topic they know well, and yet still trust the same publication on topics they do not know. The point is that the reputation extends only as far as the reader has personally verified, and trust beyond that is an unjustified leap. The remedy is to demand explanations and evidence rather than relying on the authority of the outlet, because in the region where knowledge is weakest, authority is no substitute for understanding.

A defence of inequality as the necessary condition of freedom, conditional on two provisos: that the rules under which wealth is acquired are fair, and that those who genuinely cannot cope are cared for. Given those conditions, inequality is 'not only just, it is necessary to freedom itself'. The argument is that the freedom to rise — to make a success of one's life and acquire wealth — entails that outcomes will differ, because people apply unequal effort, talent, and luck. Equality of opportunity is compatible with, and indeed requires, inequality of result; the alternative is equality of outcome purchased by the destruction of freedom.

🔑 Key Takeaway

A careful demarcation of the scope of 'the Pattern' — the recurring structure of anti-Western hatred that Deutsch and ToKTeacher identify. ToKTeacher distinguishes three claims: not every behaviour is explained by the Pattern; but every person is subject to it, including Jews; yet not everything they do is dictated by it. The point is to reject two opposite errors at once: the error of treating the Pattern as an all-consuming cause of everything (which would rob individuals of agency and dignity), and the error of denying it any explanatory role at all. Understanding a widespread phenomenon as a systematic structure need not and must not reduce every individual action to that structure — a person retains agency within it. Precision about the scope of an explanation is what keeps it from becoming a reductive or fatalistic slogan, and that precision is itself a demand for hard-to-vary, honest explanation.

David Deutsch @DavidDeutschOxf
🗓️ Published on October 25, 2026

"The process is the punishment. https://t.co/CZva8OMA4f"

🔑 Key Takeaway

A compressed observation about how institutions coerce through procedure rather than verdict. Deutsch's line 'the process is the punishment' points to the way a legal or bureaucratic mechanism can destroy a person through the sheer burden, delay, and attrition of the process itself, regardless of its outcome. The point is that power need not convict to punish: it can weaponise procedure — endless investigation, ruinous legal costs, reputational damage, uncertainty that paralyses a life or a business — so that the target is destroyed before any judgement is ever reached. This is a form of coercion that is all the more insidious for being deniable, because no one 'officially' did anything. Understanding it is the first step to resisting it, because a process that is itself the punishment cannot be defended by pointing to a fair outcome that never comes.

David Deutsch @DavidDeutschOxf
🗓️ Published on October 25, 2026

"@JuwayriyyahAlam @realzoestrimpel @Telegraph All theories of the origin of 'antisemitism' are unfalsifiable. It's not true that a non-falsifiable theory cannot be taken seriously. Philosophical theories are prime examples. It's all about hard-to-vary explanations. The issue is addressed in the essay you haven't read."

🔑 Key Takeaway

A correction of a widespread misconception about falsifiability. Deutsch's reply is that all theories of the origin of antisemitism are unfalsifiable, but that it is not true that a non-falsifiable theory cannot be taken seriously — philosophical theories are prime examples. The point is a subtle one: Popper's demarcation criterion (falsifiability as what separates science from non-science) is often wrongly treated as a veto on taking a theory seriously at all. Deutsch's refinement is that what matters is not falsifiability per se but whether an explanation is hard to vary — tightly constrained by the requirement to account for the phenomenon. A philosophical theory can be serious and testable-in-the-broader-sense without being empirically falsifiable. The deeper lesson is that the criterion of a good explanation, in any domain, is not its susceptibility to direct empirical refutation but its specificity and its vulnerability to rational criticism.

"@Ringo496351 People rarely change their minds by being proved wrong by experience. But usually they literally have no choice. What they need is somewhere to jump to, and that comes from explanation not from evidence alone. But in this case the elites maintain a taboo against such explanation."

🔑 Key Takeaway

A statement of the Popperian theory of belief change. Deutsch's reply to @Ringo496351 is that people rarely change their minds by being proved wrong by experience, but usually they literally have no choice — what they need is somewhere to jump to, and that comes from explanation, not from evidence alone. The point is that evidence can refute a belief but cannot by itself supply a replacement: a person abandons a theory only when a better one is available to jump to. The deeper claim is that the reason so many refuse to abandon destructive beliefs is not that the evidence is lacking but that the elites maintain a taboo against the explanations that would give them somewhere to jump. The obstacle to progress is not ignorance but the suppression of better explanations.
