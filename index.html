David Deutsch @DavidDeutschOxf
🗓️ Published on August 1, 2026

"Dreyfus was accused of treason because he was a Jew, and convicted on the basis of a letter implausibly being certified as being in his disguised handwriting. The real traitor was later identified – and acquited after the High Command suppressed the evidence against him. The 'prejudice' explanation – that they were so prejudiced against Jews that they believed Dreyfus must be the traitor regardless of evidence – runs into the question: why didn't they try to ban Jews from the army? There were ten Jewish generals at the time. As for the 'hatred' explanation, is it plausible that the High Command hated Jews more than they hated traitors to France – that they would themselves go to traitorous lengths to protect the real traitor, just to victimise one Jew? Ethnic hatred does not work like that. But such behaviour is common in the Pattern."

🔑 Key Takeaway

Deutsch uses the Dreyfus affair to demonstrate falsifiability in practice: the 'prejudice' and 'hatred' explanations are both refuted by the evidence — the French High Command protected the actual traitor at treasonous cost, which neither pure prejudice nor hatred predicts. He points toward a deeper explanation ('the Pattern') that accounts for institutional self-preservation overriding truth-seeking. A masterclass in applying critical rationalism to historical analysis.

Margaret Thatcher @realmrsthatcher
🗓️ Published on August 1, 2026

"People feel very resentful that if they work hard too much is taken away in tax. You know, industry won't keep going unless the people who are highly skilled feel that the skill pays in their net take home pay—and it doesn't. [...] One of the purposes of reducing tax on the pay packet is to persuade people to earn more. By taking less out of more pounds, you can still sometimes get the extra yield. [...] So you must not ever look at the British economy as if we produce a fixed amount and no more. The object of this is to start to grow again."

🔑 Key Takeaway

Thatcher argues against viewing the economy as a zero-sum pie: reducing marginal tax rates can incentivize productivity and actually increase total revenue. The core claim is dynamic — the economy is not fixed but grows in response to incentives — which remains the central fault line in tax policy debates today.

Karl Popper Quotes @QuotePopper
🗓️ Published on June 18, 2026

"The amoeba shuns falsification: its expectation is part of itself, and prescientific bearers of an expectation or a hypothesis are often destroyed by refutation of the expectation or the hypothesis. Einstein, however, made his hypothesis objective. The hypothesis is something outside him, and the scientist can destroy his hypothesis through criticism without perishing along with it. In science we get our hypotheses to die for us." —Karl Popper, All Life is Problem Solving.

🔑 Key Takeaway

Popper's most vivid metaphor for the epistemic revolution: science externalizes hypotheses so they can be killed by criticism without killing the scientist. The amoeba cannot separate itself from its expectations; the scientific tradition can. This is the deep structure of error correction — the mechanism that makes cumulative knowledge possible.
