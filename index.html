Brett Hall (ToK Teacher) @ToKTeacher

"@BrantBassamPhD Gods aren’t possible. We are out competed all the time. It’s called “the market”. As to animals: if a problem we deal with them. With other people, we reason. AI is a tool. The only other thing it can be is a person. There’s no third option. Sci-Fi scenarios are just that."

🔑 Key Takeaway

The dichotomy is exhaustive because it is drawn by the kind of explanation that applies to the thing. A tool's behaviour is accounted for entirely by its design and its circumstances, so a fault in it is a fault in its maker; a person's behaviour has to be understood by attributing knowledge and purposes, which is why disagreement with one is settled by argument. Nothing describable sits between the two, and that is why the scenario is dismissed rather than feared: a “superintelligence” that is neither a tool nor a person is not a third category of being but a phrase with no explanatory content. The remark about gods closes the opposite escape route — a being exempt from competition and criticism is not an engineering goal but an incoherence, and the market out-competing would-be gods is simply selection operating on error.

Reasoning is the only way of dealing with another person precisely because both parties are fallible explainers. Coercion can move a body; it cannot move a mind that has to be persuaded, and the fact that we can come to comprehend one another means disagreements between persons are in principle resolvable by exchanging explanations instead of force. “There’s only one kind of person” is the load-bearing clause: personhood is not a property of a substrate, so a person running on silicon would not be a new kind of entity to be feared or negotiated with, but the same kind of entity we already know how to talk to.

David Deutsch @DavidDeutschOxf
🗓️ Published on September 18, 2026

"@IMAO_ You really think it would be right to convert someone to Christianity via brain surgery? Is that what spreading the Gospel will mean?"

🔑 Key Takeaway

A single question used as a test of what a doctrine actually permits. Two answers are available. If the belief is held because it is true, the only legitimate means of transmitting it is persuasion — offering explanations a person can evaluate and reject, the one procedure that leaves the belief as knowledge in the recipient. If installing it by surgery is acceptable, that concedes the belief is being transmitted as a state of a brain rather than as an explanation, and a state that cannot be refused is indistinguishable from compulsion. The test generalises: what a position is willing to do when argument fails reveals whether it ever relied on argument.

Naval Ravikant @naval
🗓️ Published on September 18, 2026

"People who want to appear moral are attracted to the left. People who want to appear rational are attracted to the right. Effective Altruism lets you appear rational and moral - the ultimate ego trip."

🔑 Key Takeaway

The observation is about what positions get chosen for, not about which are true. Each clause is a claim about an audience: appearing moral buys standing in one coalition, appearing rational in another, and the attraction of a doctrine that certifies both at once is that it dissolves the trade-off between them. Calling the mechanism an ego trip puts the payoff in self-image — the view is held partly because holding it is flattering. The application is symmetrical and worth stating: a diagnosis that people adopt positions to signal virtue is not itself exempt from the diagnosis, and the only defence against it is the ordinary one — state the claim so it can be tested rather than displayed.

This is a claim about the conditions under which people can act on what they know, not a psychological theory. An incentive is what makes it worth someone's while to notice a better way of doing something and to bear the cost of trying it; the profit-and-loss signal is a mechanism for discovering and correcting error that is distributed across everyone rather than concentrated in a planner. The rhetoric of “the grain of human nature” understates how testable the claim is: it predicts that jurisdictions which lower the penalty for producing will accumulate producers.

An adoption problem rather than a cryptographic one. That the friction has dropped enough for onboarding to read as pleasant is the substantive report, and recommending a specific wallet is the evidence for it — a working default is what makes a guarantee real to someone who will never audit it. The significance for the underlying thesis is that a private currency's prospects are now testable on user experience rather than only on protocol claims.

🔑 Key Takeaway

A demand for consistency on a contested technical claim, in the form of a modus tollens: if one protocol's privacy guarantees were the stronger, the other would not be converging on its techniques. Whether or not the premise about copying is conceded, the structure is the point — it converts a dispute about preference into a question about which claim has to do the explanatory work, moving the burden onto the side that must import the other's machinery while denying its value. The general lesson is that competitive behaviour is evidence: what a project adopts under pressure is a more reliable signal of what it takes to be necessary than anything it says about itself.

Brett Hall (ToK Teacher) @ToKTeacher
🗓️ Published on September 17, 2026

"If the Bayesian ASI kills everyone, it dies too soon after because it’s not creative. The problem comes that’s not in its training data and hence is did not foresee (conjectuure). It also knows this, so doesn’t."

🔑 Key Takeaway

A reductio aimed at the practice of modelling an agent as a probability distribution over a fixed space of hypotheses. Bayesian updating redistributes confidence among possibilities already enumerated; it cannot manufacture a possibility nobody has thought of. Since surviving in an open-ended world depends on exactly that — anticipating a problem that is not in the training data — the imagined agent is undone not by a stronger adversary but by a gap it has no means to close. The last clause is the sharpest part: a system that understood its own inability to conjecture would know the plan fails, so it would not attempt it. Premise and prediction cancel each other.

"I wrote such a program in the 1980s on the BBC Micro, with additional frills like disabling Escape and having it beg for its life. I was trying to demonstrate the philosophical absurdity of doing what is depicted in the cartoon and what is happening in real life now. https://t.co/2UfJMrzFN7"

🔑 Key Takeaway

A demonstration from the 1980s that piling on behaviour does not produce a mind. The extra features — trapping the user, having the program plead — are the interesting part, because they are exactly the anthropomorphic signals an observer uses to infer that something is a subject, and they were trivially the programmer's design. The absurdity is structural: a specified function can include “beg for its life” while the thing performing it has nothing to lose. The second half of the sentence is why this is not merely historical — the same inference from lifelike capability to inner life is drawn today, with far more elaborate props.
