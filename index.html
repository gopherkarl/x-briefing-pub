🔑 Key Takeaway

A darkly satirical extension of the politics-of-AI-doom to its logical end. If the dominant anxiety is that an AGI/ASI is a being that could out-compete and dispossess us, and that anxiety is held by an authoritarian political tendency, then the consistent response to the arrival of such a being is not regulation but destruction - and ToKTeacher notes that the same people calling for such control would never accept the plain moral term for 'terminating' a conscious intelligence, murder. The satirical force is in closing the gap between the euphemism ('manage the risk', 'capability control', 'shut it down') and the act. It is a test of consistency: anyone who treats the ASI as an entity deserving moral consideration only when it serves their fear narrative, but as a thing to be destroyed when it arrives, is not reasoning about an agent at all - they are reasoning about an enemy.

An observation about the stability of the strongest arguments against AI-doom over more than a decade of dramatic technological change. Though the models have grown enormously since 'Superintelligence' was written, none of the core arguments - about the nature of intelligence, of knowledge, of what an agent is, of why mere scale or speed does not constitute understanding - have needed updating. This is a live example of a 'good explanation' in Deutsch's sense: the ideas that survive are hard to vary because they pin down something real about the epistemology of agency, whereas the surface-level technological predictions (exact timelines, benchmark numbers) are the parts that were always disposable. The value of revisiting the old review is precisely to separate which claims were genuinely explanatory and which were merely fashionable at the time.

"Made for people 'critical of Israel but open-minded'. There may be vanishingly few such people by now. But still: https://t.co/zFne1FJMJ3"

🔑 Key Takeaway

A pointed remark on the collapse of open-minded criticism into closed-minded condemnation. Deutsch addresses those who are genuinely 'critical of Israel but open-minded' - people whose criticism is a live inquiry open to correction rather than a settled verdict - and wryly doubts that such people still exist in sufficient number. The sting is a commentary on how political topics have become epistemically closed: to hold a position is increasingly treated as coextensive with a moral identity, so that open-minded inquiry into the evidence is rare. The deeper epistemology is that genuine criticism is not the same as condemnation - criticism is a conjectural stance that keeps the question alive, whereas condemnation is a fixed conclusion immune to counter-evidence - and the world has drifted from the former toward the latter.

Margaret Thatcher @realmrsthatcher
🗓️ Published on September 29, 2026

"When we can't run our own financial affairs properly, we don't command much respect in the world. And we can't be much help to our neighbour if we can't afford to look after ourselves. Inflation is not only a monetary phenomenon. It arises because some politicians lead the people to believe that they can consume more—much more—than they produce."

🔑 Key Takeaway

A diagnosis of inflation that treats it as, in part, an epistemic and moral failure rather than purely a monetary one. The 'monetary phenomenon' framing (inflation as too much money chasing too few goods) is not denied but deepened: the root is that politicians sell the electorate the belief that they can consume far more than they produce - a promise that contradicts arithmetic. The error is epistemic because it asserts a false relationship between consumption and production as if it were a free choice rather than a constraint of reality; the moral failure is that those leaders who know the limits exploit the public's ignorance. Nationally it is a warning that honest self-management is the foundation of both credibility abroad and the capacity to help others - you cannot aid your neighbour while bankrupting yourself.

Naval Ravikant @naval
🗓️ Published on September 28, 2026

"No one can fill the hole left by another human."

🔑 Key Takeaway

A compressed existential claim about the irreplaceability of persons. The point is not that we cannot sympathise with or be helped by others when we lose someone, but that the specific agent who is gone - with their particular knowledge, judgements, way of attending to the world - cannot be substituted by any replacement or by any amount of generic comfort. This is the personhood view pressed into the register of loss: a human is a unique source of knowledge and creative agency, so the absence of one is a deficit that no stand-in, however capable, can exactly fill, because the missing individual was solving problems in their own irreproducible way. It is a quiet argument that persons are not interchangeable units but irreplaceable sources of explanation and choice.

David Deutsch @DavidDeutschOxf
🗓️ Published on September 28, 2026

"https://t.co/HRxvR3JuCR The claim that Switzerland's sending back thousands of Jews during the Holocaust was "a move designed to keep Nazi Germany from invading" is the BBC recycling a threadbare excuse rejected by the subsequent Bergier Commission."

🔑 Key Takeaway

An instance of the correction of a historical explanation - and of how a bad story can survive despite being refuted. The BBC's framing casts Swiss complicity as an act of self-preservation forced by circumstance, but Deutsch notes that the Bergier Commission (the official Swiss fact-finding body) later rejected this very excuse. The larger epistemological point is that narratives which exonerate the comfortable can persist on the strength of being convenient and widely repeated, far outliving the evidence assembled against them; the work of criticism is to keep re-presenting the refutation. It also illustrates that 'explanation' is not decoration but the thing that determines whether we understand an event as a tragedy of moral failure or as a neutral given - and that the better explanation is the one that survives scrutiny rather than the one that flatters.

Margaret Thatcher @realmrsthatcher
🗓️ Published on September 28, 2026

"Our weaknesses are not inevitable. Our errors are not irreparable. Our sickness is not terminal. It is a crisis of the spirit, not a depletion of resources, that cripples us. Ours is the superior system, the victorious creed, the best, indeed the only, hope for mankind."

🔑 Key Takeaway

The assertion that a civilisation's decline is a failure of will and ideas, not of physical endowment. Weakness, error and sickness are re-described not as fated states but as problems to be solved: because knowledge can always be created, no limitation is in principle permanent, and a 'depletion of resources' is secondary to a 'crisis of the spirit' - a collapse of the belief that problems are soluble. This is a Deutschian diagnosis in the voice of statecraft: decline is not the exhaustion of material but the abandonment of the optimistic, problem-solving stance that generated prosperity in the first place. 'The superior system... the only hope' is the corollary claim - that the free, error-correcting civilisation, when it stops apologising for itself and recovers the conviction that its own principles are true, is the one that keeps solving problems.
